The most important number in OpenAI's latest round isn't $1.25 trillion. It's $19 billion, the gap between what OpenAI went out to raise and what it actually raised.

Read the headlines and you see a record: $11 billion at a $1.25T valuation, more than four times the $300 billion mark from SoftBank's $40 billion round in March 2025. Read the order book and you see something else. The most famous AI company in the world asked for $30 billion and filled about 37% of it. Investors still showed up. Plenty of them looked at the price and stayed home.

My view is that the market hasn't turned on AI. It has turned on one particular way of building an AI company. Capital is still flowing. It is just getting much pickier about where it goes, and the dividing line is efficiency, not hype.

## The Valuation Ladder Ran Out of Rungs

Look at how fast the ladder went up: $300 billion in March 2025, about $500 billion in the October 2025 secondary sale, and now $1.25T. Each step depended on new buyers believing the next step would come. At $1.25T, the pool of people who can write a check worth having gets very small. You are down to sovereign wealth funds, a few mega-funds, and strategic investors who sell OpenAI chips and cloud capacity and then invest some of that revenue back.

That last group matters most. When your supplier is also your investor, part of your valuation is really a discount on what you buy from them. Getting only $11 billion suggests the investors who would have bought purely for financial returns have mostly done the math and passed.

Cisco is the obvious historical comparison. It hit a $555 billion market cap in March 2000 and really was the backbone of the internet. The business was real. The problem was that the price had already counted a decade of perfect execution. OpenAI's products are real too: ChatGPT is the most widely used consumer AI product on the planet. That doesn't prevent the price from getting ahead of the business.

## Anthropic's S-1 Changed What a "Good" AI Company Looks Like

The timing hurts OpenAI because Anthropic just published its numbers. The S-1 shows first-half 2026 revenue of **$16.3 billion**, up from **$1.2 billion** in the first half of 2025. That is about 13x year over year. Trailing revenue per employee was **over $6.6 million** as of September 30, 2026, up from $1.4 million a year earlier. Anthropic grew that number even though its headcount roughly tripled.

Here is the angle almost nobody is covering. **Anthropic's IPO is the biggest threat to OpenAI's private valuation, and the reason has nothing to do with market share.** The threat is price discovery. Right now OpenAI's $1.25T is a number negotiated in a room. Once Anthropic trades publicly, every fund manager gets a live, daily, liquid price for a frontier lab. If public investors put Anthropic at, say, 25x forward revenue, OpenAI's backers will have to explain why their asset should trade at a much higher multiple. Private marks can ignore public comparables until a public comparable shows up in the same category.

For three years, frontier labs were valued on stories about AGI timelines. From now on they will be valued against a ticker.

## Revenue Per Employee Is the New Valuation Anchor

The efficiency gap shows up well beyond the frontier labs. ICONIQ's benchmarks put the typical B2B software company at **$237K of ARR per employee**. The top-growth companies above $100M ARR run at **$655K** while still growing 115%. Compare that with the outliers:

- **AppLovin**: $1.92B of revenue in Q2 2026 with 876 full-time employees. That is about **$8.8M per FTE**, with an 84% adjusted EBITDA margin.
- **Palantir**: $2.31B in Q3 2026, up 96%, with 4,634 employees and a 62% adjusted operating margin.
- **Base44**: started by one founder in January 2025 with roughly $10,000–20,000 of his own money. It reached $1M ARR in three weeks and sold to Wix for $80M in cash after six months, when it had **8 employees**. It now reportedly runs at **$200M ARR**.

Base44 is the story that should bother late-stage investors. A bootstrapped side project reached a nine-figure run rate inside about 18 months, on top of the foundation models these mega-rounds pay for. That is the uncomfortable second-order effect. The labs absorb the capital and the burn, while the companies building on top capture margin with tiny teams. Gartner expects spending on GenAI models to grow **117%** in 2026, so model revenue is real. But Gartner also says much of software's 14.6% growth comes from price increases, meaning the same seats sold at higher prices plus AI add-ons. Price increases are a one-time boost. They don't compound.

## The 2021 Hangover Is Coming Due

The other half of the fatigue story is quieter and more telling. Ada was valued at $1.2B in the pandemic era. It just merged into Dialpad in an all-stock deal valued at about **US$430 million**. No shareholder gets cash. That headline number depends on Dialpad's own **$2.2 billion valuation from December 2021**, a price nobody has tested in almost five years. For Ada's stake to actually be worth $430M, the combined company has to be worth about 5.6x its $470M ARR. Public comparables like RingCentral (~3x) and Five9 (~2x) trade far below that.

Compare that with Salesforce buying Cresta for a reported **$2.7 billion**, which gives investors public stock or cash. The market is splitting in two. AI-native companies that are clearly winning get real exits. Everyone else gets private-to-private stock swaps that relabel the losses and call them a merger.

Big companies are adjusting too. In September, Microsoft folded Microsoft Research's AI Frontiers group, around 50 researchers behind Autogen and the Fara computer-use agent, into Microsoft AI. The group's lead has written publicly that compute availability was often a severe bottleneck. When even Microsoft is merging AI teams and rationing GPUs, you are not looking at a world of unlimited AI money. You are looking at money being rationed.

## The Bear Case Against My Bear Case

To be fair: $11 billion is still one of the largest private raises in history, bigger than almost any IPO. OpenAI may have structured the round in tranches, held back on purpose to limit dilution, or be planning to bring in strategic money later. Gartner also projects data center systems spending of **$1.366 trillion in 2027**, up 55.5%. Demand for compute is real and still rising. And the "AI bubble" call has been wrong every quarter since 2023.

All of that is true, and it doesn't undo the signal. A company with OpenAI's brand doesn't miss a target by 63% voluntarily. And demand for data centers says nothing about who earns money on those data centers. The railroads were real, and many railroad investors still lost everything.

## What to Watch

1. **Anthropic's IPO multiple sets the ceiling.** I expect Anthropic to price below the revenue multiple implied by OpenAI's $1.25T. Within two quarters, at least one large OpenAI investor will quietly mark its position down.
2. **OpenAI fills the $19B gap with structured money, not straight equity.** Expect debt, vendor financing, or compute-for-equity deals with chip and cloud partners before the end of Q1 2027. Read that as a symptom of the gap, not a solution to it.
3. **Revenue per employee becomes a standard metric in late-stage pitch decks.** By mid-2027, Series C+ companies below $500K ARR per employee will struggle to raise at flat valuations.
4. **More stock-for-stock deals like Ada.** Expect at least five more private-private mergers among 2021-vintage AI and SaaS companies in the next six months, mostly in CX, sales tools, and dev tools.
5. **More Big Tech research labs get folded into product orgs.** Microsoft won't be the last. Wherever compute is scarce, open-ended research loses to work that ships.

The AI boom is fine. What's ending is the era when every check written to a frontier lab was assumed to be a good one.
