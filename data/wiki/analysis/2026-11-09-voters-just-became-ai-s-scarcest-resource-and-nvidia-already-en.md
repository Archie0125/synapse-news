For two years, the AI industry said its bottleneck was chips. Then it said power. After November 3, 2026, the bottleneck is people, and you can't place a backorder for them.

The numbers are lopsided. Tom's Hardware counted about 45 data center measures on local ballots this cycle. Voters approved restrictions or moratoriums in 40 of the 41 that would limit them. Very few issues produce results like that, and only when voters feel personally threatened. The losses also went well beyond ballot questions. In Michigan's 7th District, Will Lawrence is a Sunrise Movement co-founder who made data centers the center of his campaign months before most candidates noticed the issue. He beat incumbent Republican Tom Barrett in one of the most expensive House races in the country, even though Elon Musk's America PAC spent $340,000 against him. In Texas, the state that built its pitch around cheap land, cheap power and no questions asked, Gina Hinojosa defeated Greg Abbott after promising to restrict data centers.

Across the Atlantic, Amsterdam police arrested 150 to 200 protesters at a Microsoft data center construction site run by Pure Data Centers. Police seized break-in tools and reported finding items allegedly stolen from the site. Organizers say Amsterdam is the first of 85 planned actions under the "Stop Dirty Data Centers" banner, with Dublin, Warsaw and Denmark next.

So the opposition now shows up in three forms: ballots, courtrooms and people climbing fences. Capital can't buy its way past any of them.

### Why now, and why it's different from 2019

This is not the first data center backlash. Amsterdam paused new data center construction in 2019. In 2022 the Netherlands killed Meta's planned hyperscale campus in Zeewolde after public anger over farmland and power. Ireland, where data centers used about 21% of national electricity in 2023, has effectively blocked new grid connections in the Dublin region until around 2028.

What has changed is the scale of the projects and who ends up paying. The 2019 fights were about sites drawing tens of megawatts. Today's AI campuses are planned in gigawatts. In Michigan, Lawrence says proposed projects would use double or triple the state grid's total capacity. Utility bills have also turned an abstract worry into a household one. PJM, the grid operator covering 13 states from Virginia to Illinois, saw its 2025/26 capacity auction clear at $269.92 per megawatt-day, up from $28.92 the year before. That cost reaches residential bills. In 2023, Lawrence Berkeley National Lab estimated data centers used 4.4% of US electricity and projected 6.7% to 12% by 2028.

When a voter's power bill goes up and a windowless building 2 million square feet across appears next to the school, nobody needs to explain the connection. Lawrence put it plainly: the election was about "costs and corruption," and data centers fit both. They receive tax breaks, draw down water and sue the towns that try to regulate them.

### Musk is the warning, not the outlier

The most expensive lesson of this cycle belongs to Elon Musk. As TechCrunch's Sean O'Kane noted, Musk gave about half a billion dollars to Republicans. In return his companies are headquartered in a Texas that just turned blue, and his data center operations are associated in the public mind with exactly what voters rejected. xAI's Mississippi site runs on converted jet-engine turbines under permits that allow 25% higher pollutant limits. Dutch protesters named it in their manifesto. One data center in Mississippi became a talking point at a protest in Amsterdam.

That's what makes the backlash dangerous for the industry: it travels. Each badly handled project becomes material for the next local fight somewhere else. Hyperscalers have treated siting as a hundred separate real-estate deals. Voters now treat it as one story about one industry.

### What nobody is talking about: the backlash is redesigning AI infrastructure

Most coverage reads this as a slowdown story. I think the more important change is architectural.

Look at what Nvidia is doing. According to The Information, it has pitched Apollo Global Management on converting about 130 old Brightspeed telecom buildings into small data centers. Combined, they could deliver about 1 gigawatt, or more than 2,200 Vera Rubin NVL72 racks. These buildings already have power infrastructure, already sit in neighborhoods, and already passed zoning decades ago. They still need cooling upgrades and local permits, but nobody holds a town hall about a telephone exchange that has stood on Main Street since 1975. OpenAI and Anthropic are reportedly looking for smaller data center deals for the same reason.

The second-order effect is that local consent is turning into a priced asset. Companies that already hold permitted, powered, unremarkable real estate, like telcos, utilities, old industrial owners and private equity firms such as Apollo, have something a gigawatt greenfield campus can't buy. Apollo is also part of a reported $40 billion debt package for SpaceX covering about 360,000 Vera Rubin GPUs. It needs places to plug those chips in now, not after a three-year zoning fight. I expect 2027's hottest infrastructure trade to be a dull asset with a power hookup and no history of controversy.

There's a darker version too. Tom's Hardware reported that Amazon used 45-year-old rules in Gilroy, California, to keep residents out of the public comment window for a large project. When consent is expensive, some companies will choose to avoid asking for it. That works once. Then it ends up in the next campaign ad.

### The permitting bill is the real battlefield

Washington is moving in the opposite direction from voters. A bipartisan permitting reform bill, pushed by a "four corners" group of senators including Shelley Moore Capito and Sheldon Whitehouse, could speed up energy projects and lower utility bills. It would do that partly by limiting environmental review and restricting communities' ability to stop projects in court. NRDC's Christy Goldfuss warned that under the bill, "data centers would not need to answer to the concerns of local communities."

That puts the two sides in direct conflict. Voters just said they want more local control. A lame-duck Congress may hand developers less local friction. If it passes before January, the industry gets a federal shield right when local hostility is peaking. Expect the next fight to be over preemption, with local measures challenged as conflicting with federal energy policy.

### The bear case

Here's the honest counterargument: none of this stops AI buildout. It moves it. Of the 3,000-plus US counties, the 40 measures cover a small share. Capital will go to friendlier states, behind-the-meter gas sites, and abroad, including the planned 5-gigawatt Stargate UAE campus in Abu Dhabi, where there are no ballot measures. Hyperscaler capex is running at roughly $400 billion a year, and that money won't disappear because Ann Arbor voted no. Kirsten Korosec herself said data centers probably weren't the main driver of the blue wave.

All true. But relocation has a cost. Every move adds months, more expensive power or geopolitical risk. And demand migrating offshore is itself a political problem for an industry that has spent two years telling Washington that AI dominance requires building at home. You can't argue national security and then put the compute in the Gulf.

### What to Watch

1. **The first "ratepayer protection" mandate in a major state, by mid-2027.** Texas under Hinojosa is the likeliest candidate. Expect rules requiring large loads to pay for their own grid upgrades, so residential customers stop covering them. Other states will copy it within a year.
2. **OpenAI, Microsoft and Google will announce formal community commitments covering water caps, tariff guarantees and local revenue sharing before Q2 2027.** TechCrunch already notes OpenAI suddenly saying "we can change our policies." Look for these to be written into contracts, not just press releases.
3. **Telco and brownfield retrofits will make up a growing share of new US AI capacity announcements.** The Nvidia–Brightspeed deal is the template. Watch Lumen, Frontier and private equity owners of old industrial sites.
4. **The permitting bill will pass in some form. Its local-court restrictions will be the first thing litigated, and the first thing Democrats try to roll back** once they control the House in January.
5. **European protests will escalate, and Ireland will extend its Dublin restrictions** instead of loosening them on schedule.

The AI industry spent years optimizing FLOPS per watt. The next optimization target is megawatts per angry voter. Companies that work that out first will get to build. The rest will be stuck in court.
