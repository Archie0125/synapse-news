The most important AI hardware deal of the month has nothing to do with chips. Anthropic's $3.4 billion gas turbine deal tells you more about where this industry is going than any GPU launch this year. A company built on frontier models, constitutional AI and safety research is now effectively in the power plant business. That's not a side story. It's the main one.

For three years, the AI story has been about compute: who has the H100s, who has the Blackwells, who has the TPU allocation. That story is ending. Chips can be bought, even when they're scarce. Electrons, delivered to a specific substation on a specific date, cannot. And last week, American voters made it clear that they will decide who gets them.

## The bottleneck moved, and the money followed

Look at where capital is flowing. Geothermal startup Fervo reported $11.9 billion in contracted revenue in its Q3 earnings on Monday, up from $7.2 billion a quarter earlier. That's a 65% jump in three months for a company still losing money ($43.7 million operating loss last quarter). Fervo connected its first 100 MW plant to the grid this year and is building a 396 MW plant for Google at Cape Station in Utah, due by the end of 2028.

Meanwhile, the gas turbine market has turned into a seller's market. Tom's Hardware has reported delivery estimates for some US builders stretching to 2030. SpaceX has started making its own turbine blades for Musk's gas-powered AI facilities, which reportedly cuts generator delays by 18 months. OpenAI's first Stargate site in Abilene, Texas (about 820 MW of critical IT capacity) is also deploying on-site gas turbines. When a rocket company starts machining turbine blades, the supply chain is broken.

So Anthropic's $3.4 billion makes sense. If you can't wait for the utility, and you can't wait in line at GE Vernova or Siemens Energy, you buy your own generation. It's the same logic that pushed Microsoft toward restarting Three Mile Island and pushed Amazon toward small modular reactors. The difference is speed: gas can be up in two to three years. Nuclear takes a decade.

## The backlash is about bills, not culture

Big Tech wants you to believe the data center fight is a culture war between people who want progress and people who don't. That framing is self-serving and mostly wrong.

MIT Technology Review's James O'Donnell spent time in Ashburn, Virginia, the heart of "Data Center Alley" and the biggest data center hub in the world. His conclusion: the real fault line is electricity. When data centers draw power from the grid, they push up everyone's rates. When they generate power onsite, they bring noise, emissions and turbines into people's backyards. Each choice causes a different kind of anger, but both are about power.

The numbers back him up. PJM, the grid operator covering Virginia and 12 other states, saw its capacity auction price jump from about $29 per MW-day for 2024/25 to about $270 for 2025/26, a roughly ninefold increase driven largely by projected data center demand. Those costs end up on household bills. A family in Maryland doesn't care about recursive self-improvement. They care that their electric bill went up while a building with no windows went up down the road.

That explains last week's midterms. According to TechCrunch, dozens of local ballot measures to restrict or pause data centers almost all passed. In Texas, the state that sold itself as the friendliest place on earth to build energy infrastructure, Gina Hinojosa beat Greg Abbott for governor after campaigning openly on restricting data centers. Elon Musk gave Republicans roughly half a billion dollars and now runs his companies from a state that just elected a governor who wants to slow down his Colossus-style buildouts. Musk is a big data center builder, and his money bought him close to nothing.

Europe is going the same way. In Thurrock, 15 miles east of London, a petition with almost 55,000 signatures asks Google to drop a planned data center. The coalition is wonderfully strange: Buglife, which is worried about a rare bumblebee; the legal nonprofit Foxglove, which is worried about pollution; and the Thurrock Hammers motorcycle club, which wants its racing track back. In Amsterdam, protesters climbed a crane at a Microsoft site. When bikers and entomologists are on the same side, you've lost the room.

## Here's what nobody is talking about: onsite gas walks into the next backlash

The industry's answer to grid backlash is "we'll make our own power, so we won't raise your bills." It sounds smart, and it's a trap.

Onsite gas doesn't make the political problem go away. It moves it from the utility bill to the local air permit. xAI already showed how this goes in Memphis, where gas turbines at its Colossus site caused months of fights over emissions and permits with nearby residents. Grid power makes you the villain on everyone's bill. Onsite gas makes you the villain in the neighborhood air. Either way you have a villain, and voters now know who it is.

There's a second problem: the economics only work while AI labs don't care about price. Fervo's numbers are useful here. Its first Cape Station phase cost $7,000 per kilowatt; the Google project is down to $5,500. With subsidies, that's roughly comparable to a new gas plant burning $6/MMBtu fuel, which is still about twice current gas prices. Hyperscalers will pay those premiums today because a GPU sitting idle waiting for power is the most expensive asset in the building. Once the cluster race calms down and finance teams start asking about cost per token, every dollar per kilowatt matters again.

## China's quiet advantage

The comparison Americans should worry about is in Inner Mongolia. According to SemiAnalysis, Envision built the first 120 MW of its Galaxy campus in Ulanqab in less than a year, at roughly one-fifth the cost of comparable US projects. It plans to reach 800 MW of IT capacity by the end of 2027, close to Stargate Abilene's scale.

Envision isn't waiting for turbines. It's building 420 MW of wind farms against a 240 MW load for the first two phases, oversizing because Ulanqab's wind capacity factor is about 35–40%, and adding four-hour battery storage. Inside, solid-state transformers convert 10kV AC directly to 800V DC for the racks. China's advantage isn't only cheap labor. It's that a remote region with strong wind, little local opposition and a government that wants this to happen can turn power into compute with no ballot measure in the way.

The US is building AI capacity in suburbs where people vote. China is building it in grasslands where they mostly don't. That asymmetry matters more than any chip export rule.

## The bear case

To be fair: the backlash may be overstated. TechCrunch's Kirsten Korosec said herself that data centers probably weren't the main driver of the blue wave. Many of the ballot measures were local and narrow. Capital is mobile, and states like Wyoming, North Dakota and Louisiana will happily take projects that Loudoun County or Texas turns away. Efficiency gains, like DeepSeek-style training and better inference chips, could also cut power demand per unit of AI work.

All of that is true, and none of it changes the direction. Moving to remote states means building new transmission, and transmission is the slowest and most politically contested infrastructure in America. Efficiency gains historically lead to more total use, not less (Jevons paradox has held up for every major computing wave so far). And the backlash is now organized, bipartisan and international.

## What to Watch

1. **"Bring your own power" becomes a legal requirement, not a marketing line.** Expect at least two US states, with Texas at the top of the list once Hinojosa takes office, to require large data centers to supply or pay for dedicated generation in 2027. Ratepayer-protection tariffs like the large-load rules already in place at some utilities will spread.
2. **More AI labs sign turbine deals in the next six months.** Anthropic won't be the last. OpenAI, xAI and Meta will lock up turbine supply, pushing turbine lead times further out and making firms like Fervo look like a hedge.
3. **Geothermal and storage valuations keep climbing.** Fervo's contracted backlog will pass $15 billion by mid-2027, and it will raise money or go public at a valuation that would have seemed absurd two years ago.
4. **Scott Wiener links AI safety to energy.** The newly elected representative for Pelosi's old seat already talks about slowing AI down. The easiest path in Congress isn't an AI safety bill; it's energy and ratepayer protection. Watch for that framing.
5. **The cost gap with China becomes a lobbying weapon.** The Galaxy campus's "one-fifth the cost" number will be used in Washington to argue for overriding local permits. That will make the local backlash worse, not better.

The AI industry spent three years thinking its main constraint was Jensen Huang's production schedule. It's now finding out that the real constraint is a county board meeting on a Tuesday night.
