Nvidia’s biggest problem is not that Jensen Huang resigned. It is that a company supplying the computational infrastructure of the AI economy apparently needed a sex scandal, an alleged Chinese intelligence connection, and congressional pressure before its board remembered how to govern.

Huang’s departure is unusually destabilizing because he was more than Nvidia’s CEO. He was chief product narrator, customer closer, Washington lobbyist, engineering referee, and walking share-price multiplier. Apple lost Steve Jobs in 2011 after Tim Cook had already run day-to-day operations during multiple medical leaves. Amazon replaced Jeff Bezos with Andy Jassy, the executive who built AWS. Nvidia has handed the company to CFO Colette Kress on an interim basis while beginning an internal and external search. That is not succession. It is emergency containment.

The numbers explain why this matters beyond Silicon Valley gossip. Nvidia generated $130.5 billion in revenue in fiscal 2025, more than double the previous year, while data-center revenue reached $115.2 billion. Net income was nearly $72.9 billion. Industry estimates have regularly placed Nvidia’s share of the AI accelerator market above 80 percent. When one company controls that much of the hardware used by Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, Google, Oracle, CoreWeave, and scores of AI labs, a leadership failure becomes an infrastructure event.

Investors understood that immediately. Nvidia shares fell more than 10 percent after the resignation announcement and were down over 23 percent for the month, according to Tom’s Hardware. At Nvidia’s scale, that is roughly $1 trillion in erased market value—not because a factory burned down or Blackwell stopped working, but because the market could no longer price the judgment of one man as a dependable corporate asset.

That is the first lesson: Nvidia’s famous founder premium has become a founder discount.

### The board’s failure started before the resignation

The allegations are serious. Huang admitted to an affair with Alice Huang, a businesswoman whom US officials reportedly suspect of being connected to Chinese intelligence. Her company, Megaspeed, has been linked to an investigation concerning as much as $2 billion in restricted Nvidia chips allegedly routed toward China. Separate reporting alleges another relationship with Nvidia healthcare executive Kimberly Powell, who reported to him.

Those facts raise three different governance questions. Did Huang expose himself to coercion? Did personal relationships affect commercial or personnel decisions? And when did Nvidia’s directors learn enough to investigate?

The last question may prove most damaging. WIRED reported that Huang hired a crisis communications firm in 2025 and that the firm was being included on press inquiries involving his personal life by October of that year. If outside advisers were preparing for fallout while the board remained passive—or knew and failed to disclose the risk—then this was not a sudden crisis. It was a delayed one.

Nvidia’s directors eventually removed the founder, but boards do not earn medals for pulling the fire alarm after smoke fills the building. Their job was to prevent one executive’s private conduct from becoming a risk to export compliance, employee trust, government relations, and nearly $1 trillion in shareholder value.

The alleged relationship with a direct report makes the problem worse. Even without a national-security element, a CEO’s affair with an employee in his reporting chain creates questions about consent, promotion, compensation, retaliation, and internal investigations. Nvidia’s board must examine personnel decisions surrounding that relationship, not merely declare that Huang’s resignation “closes the matter.”

### Colette Kress is credible—and structurally overloaded

Kress is the sensible interim choice. She joined Nvidia in 2013, helped manage its transformation from a gaming-chip company into the dominant AI infrastructure supplier, and has enough Wall Street credibility to answer questions without imitating Huang’s leather-jacket mythology. Before Nvidia, she spent 25 years across Cisco, Microsoft, and Texas Instruments.

But the board has also left her serving as both interim CEO and CFO. That may work for several weeks; it becomes irresponsible if allowed to drift for quarters. During a disclosure, compliance, and national-security crisis, the CEO needs an independent finance chief challenging assumptions, protecting controls, and separating operational ambition from what the company can safely tell investors.

Kress also inherits decisions that are not primarily financial. Nvidia must allocate scarce Blackwell systems, negotiate with TSMC over advanced packaging, manage cloud customers that are simultaneously building rival chips, and defend its China strategy in Washington. A gifted CFO can understand a product roadmap. That does not automatically make her the person engineering teams will trust to choose between competing architectures five years out.

Nvidia should appoint an acting CFO within 90 days and disclose a timetable for selecting a permanent CEO. If it does neither, investors should assume the board still confuses continuity with preparation.

### What nobody is talking about: customers now have permission to defect

AMD, Intel, and custom-chip vendors will not suddenly match Nvidia because Huang left. CUDA, introduced in 2006, represents two decades of software, developer habits, libraries, and tooling. Nvidia also bought Mellanox for $6.9 billion in 2020, giving it networking technology needed to sell entire AI systems rather than isolated GPUs. AMD’s Instinct MI300 and later accelerators may compete on memory or price, but ROCm still asks customers to absorb switching costs. Intel’s Gaudi effort has struggled to establish the same software pull.

That is the bull case, and it is real.

Yet procurement decisions are not made only on benchmark charts. They are also shaped by concentration risk, political exposure, supply certainty, and executive confidence. Before this scandal, a cloud executive proposing to move workloads away from Nvidia had to explain why the company should sacrifice CUDA compatibility. Now the same executive can ask why 90 percent of an AI fleet should depend on a supplier with an untested CEO, an open congressional investigation, and a board that missed its founder problem.

Google has TPUs. Amazon has Trainium and Inferentia. Microsoft has Maia. Meta is developing MTIA. Broadcom and Marvell help hyperscalers design custom accelerators. None needs to replace Nvidia outright. Moving even 5 to 10 percentage points of future inference capacity to in-house silicon would redirect billions of dollars and weaken Nvidia’s pricing power.

That is the second-order effect rivals can exploit: not a mass migration, but a change in what procurement committees consider responsible.

### China has turned from a market problem into a trust problem

Nvidia reported that customers billed to China, including Hong Kong, represented about 13 percent of fiscal 2025 revenue. US restrictions have already forced the company to modify products such as the A800, H800, and H20. In April 2025, new licensing requirements led Nvidia to record a $5.5 billion charge tied to H20 inventory and commitments.

Huang publicly argued that export controls could accelerate Chinese alternatives such as Huawei’s Ascend chips. That argument deserved debate when it came from Nvidia’s CEO. After an admitted affair involving a person suspected of Chinese intelligence links, the same policy position will be treated in Washington as evidence, not analysis.

House committee chairs Brian Mast, John Moolenaar, and Rick Crawford demanded Huang’s testimony; he agreed to appear behind closed doors by Thanksgiving after lawmakers threatened a subpoena. The closed format may protect classified information, but it will not repair public confidence. Every undisclosed meeting, sales exception, and lobbying contact will now be read through a counterintelligence lens.

Nvidia can survive tighter China controls. What it cannot do is continue presenting export compliance as a policy inconvenience managed through Huang’s personal relationships in Washington.

### Taiwan will feel the scheduling shock, not the demand collapse

For Taiwan, the tempting conclusion is that Nvidia’s crisis threatens the entire AI supply chain. That overstates the immediate danger. TSMC manufactures chips for Nvidia’s competitors too. If Google, Amazon, Microsoft, or Broadcom wins more accelerator orders, much of that silicon may still be fabricated in Taiwan. AI demand does not disappear because Nvidia’s board failed.

The near-term risk sits in scheduling. Blackwell systems involve TSMC’s advanced nodes and CoWoS packaging, SK Hynix or Micron high-bandwidth memory, and rack integration by companies including Foxconn, Quanta, and Wistron. If Nvidia delays product decisions or customers revise orders, suppliers face allocation whiplash: the total market remains large, but the mix, timing, and margins change.

Taiwanese investors should therefore watch purchase commitments and packaging allocation, not every headline about Huang. The founder is gone; the bottlenecks are still physical.

### What to Watch

First, Nvidia will appoint a separate acting CFO within three months. If Kress retains both jobs into 2027, that is evidence of a thinner management bench than shareholders were led to believe.

Second, hyperscalers will raise the share of new inference capacity assigned to TPUs, Trainium, Maia, MTIA, and AMD accelerators by 5 to 10 percentage points over the next 12 to 18 months. Training will remain heavily Nvidia-dependent; inference is where defection starts.

Third, the board will create a dedicated security and export-compliance committee, publish stricter rules covering executive relationships, and add at least two directors with intelligence, defense, or semiconductor operations experience. Anything less will look cosmetic.

Fourth, Huang’s closed-door testimony will not contain the political damage. A transcript will leak, or lawmakers will demand public testimony in early 2027. Nvidia has become too important—and the allegations too politically useful—for Congress to move on quietly.

Finally, Nvidia’s next CEO will be sold as a steward, not another celebrity founder. That is the right choice. The AI industry does not need a new leather jacket. It needs proof that the company supplying its most important machines can function without treating one man as its operating system.
