Snap Inc. announced on Wednesday that it would eliminate approximately 1,000 employees, about 16% of its full-time workforce, in a restructuring that CEO Evan Spiegel tied to rapid advancements in artificial intelligence. The move also closes more than 300 open positions that will simply go unfilled, making clear that Snap is not just trimming around the edges but redesigning its operating model around a smaller, AI-augmented headcount.

The announcement sent Snap's shares up roughly 7% on Wednesday.

## An AI-Native Operating Model

In a letter to employees obtained by multiple outlets, Spiegel described an operating environment that has fundamentally changed. "Rapid advancements in artificial intelligence enable our teams to reduce repetitive work, increase velocity, and better support our community, partners, and advertisers," he wrote, framing the layoffs not as a crisis response but as a forward-looking organizational redesign.

The metrics backing that claim are striking. Snap said in an investor presentation that AI agents are already generating over 65% of its new code. The company said its AI agents also respond to over 1 million queries per month.

The restructuring is expected to reduce Snap's annualized cost base by more than $500 million by the second half of 2026. Restructuring charges of $95 million to $130 million are expected, primarily severance and related costs, contract termination costs and other impairment charges, with the majority incurred in the second quarter of 2026. Affected U.S. employees will receive four months of severance pay with continued healthcare coverage, equity vesting through the severance period, and career transition support.

## The Business Case

The layoffs are not just about AI efficiency — they are also a financial necessity. Snap has faced intensifying competition from TikTok and longtime rivals including Instagram and X, and its advertising revenue has been under pressure. The combination of AI capability gains and a leaner cost structure is management's wager that the company can reach sustainable profitability without needing to grow revenue at the pace its previous cost structure demanded.

Unlike past rounds of tech layoffs driven by macro conditions, this restructuring is explicitly framed around capability substitution — AI doing the work, not just accelerating it.

## Controversy and Coachella

The announcement landed awkwardly for Spiegel personally.

The backlash reflects a broader tension in the current wave of AI-driven layoffs: unlike cuts during economic downturns, these reductions happen while companies post rising revenues and growing profits, which makes the human cost harder for observers to frame as unfortunate-but-necessary.

## A Broader Wave

Snap joins a growing list of tech companies, including Meta, Oracle and Amazon, that have undergone significant cuts this year.

The timing also coincides with a structural shift in how software companies staff support operations.

## What Snap's Numbers Signal

What makes this announcement distinctively significant beyond its scale is the specificity of Snap's AI disclosures. Rather than offering vague language about "efficiency," the company published figures: AI agents generating over 65% of new code and responding to over 1 million queries per month. That transparency is deliberate. Snap wants investors, analysts, and peers to understand that the restructuring is backed by demonstrated AI capability — not financial desperation dressed up in innovation language.

That framing matters, because it establishes a template other mid-size consumer tech companies will study closely. When AI tooling reaches the point where a company can quantify the ratio of AI-to-human output in production systems, the organizational question shifts from "should we invest in AI?" to "how many roles can we absorb without hiring?"

The "new way of working" Spiegel described in his letter is not aspirational language. At 65% AI-generated code — and rising — it is an operating reality. The April 15 announcement was the acknowledgment of a transformation that had already happened.
