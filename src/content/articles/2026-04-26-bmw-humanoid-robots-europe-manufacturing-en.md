After an initial test deployment in December 2025, BMW plans a further test of a humanoid robot at its Leipzig plant from April 2026, part of the first pilot project with humanoid robots in Europe for the BMW Group. It is a quiet beginning to what the company believes is a fundamental shift in how physical manufacturing gets done.

The robot is called AEON, built by Hexagon Robotics, the Zurich-based Physical AI unit of Hexagon, a long-standing BMW partner in sensor technology and software. AEON has a human-like body that allows a wide range of hand and gripper elements or scanning tools to be attached, and it is used dynamically on wheels. The choice reflects a pragmatic engineering philosophy—get the robot into production first, optimize the form factor later.

## From Spartanburg to Leipzig

BMW's humanoid robot program did not begin in Europe. It began at the company's Spartanburg facility in South Carolina, where a 2025 pilot with Figure AI was the world's first deployment of humanoid robots at a BMW Group plant.

BMW reported that within ten months Figure 02 supported the production of more than 30,000 BMW X3, moved more than 90,000 components and covered about 1.2 million steps in around 1,250 operating hours. Figure 02 handled the precise removal and positioning of sheet metal parts for the welding process, a task BMW describes as demanding in terms of speed and accuracy. These are exactly the tasks humanoid robots can currently handle competently.

## The AEON Platform

The imitation learning component is notable. This dramatically reduces the programming overhead that has historically made industrial robots inflexible—a robot that needs 500 hours of software engineering to learn a new task is not deployable across the varied, rapidly-changing workflows of automotive assembly.

During testing and later in the pilot phase, the robot is to be used in the assembly of high-voltage batteries and in component manufacturing. This pairing tests both ends of the dexterity spectrum BMW expects humanoid robots to eventually address.

## Physical AI as Industrial Strategy

BMW has set up a Center of Competence for Physical AI in Production to consolidate expertise so it can be leveraged across the organisation, and to support pilot projects at its plants.

"Physical AI," as BMW uses the term, means digital AI agents combined with real machines and robots. The distinction matters because physical AI requires fundamentally different engineering: the system cannot simply predict the next token; it must pick up a part, not drop it, and avoid injuring the human working three feet away.

## Europe's Industrial Stakes

European automotive manufacturing is under structural pressure from multiple directions: the EV transition has disrupted traditional powertrain supply chains, displacing skilled workers in combustion engine assembly and creating rapid demand for battery and electronics expertise. And the slower economic growth of recent years has reduced the pricing flexibility that European premium brands historically relied on.

Humanoid robots, if deployable at scale, represent one plausible path to closing the productivity gap.

The political dimension is significant. BMW's official position frames humanoid robots as a complement to existing automation, particularly for monotonous, ergonomically demanding or safety-critical tasks, with the goal of relieving employees.

Humanoid robots at their current capability level genuinely are better suited to dull, strenuous, repetitive tasks than to skilled cognitive-physical tasks like quality inspection or complex troubleshooting. The operational case for the Leipzig pilot is real. But manufacturers and unions both understand that capability ceilings tend to rise.

## Where the Race Stands

BMW's European deployment arrives at a moment when the global humanoid robot race is accelerating across multiple fronts:

- **Figure AI** ran the Spartanburg pilot at BMW, and BMW and Figure are now evaluating additional use cases for the Figure 03 robot.
- **Agility Robotics' Digit** robots were deployed by GXO at a Spanx facility in Georgia, and Agility has signed an agreement with Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada.
- **Hyundai Motor Group** has plans to purchase thousands of Boston Dynamics' Atlas robots in the coming years.

The common thread across all of these is a shift from demonstration to deployment—robots working in live production environments rather than controlled labs. The technology has not suddenly become perfect; it has become good enough for specific, bounded tasks under careful monitoring.

BMW's Leipzig pilot is important not because AEON represents the apex of humanoid robotics, but because it represents a serious industrial organization running a disciplined experiment to understand how physical AI integrates into a real, high-stakes production environment. The lessons from Leipzig will shape how BMW scales, which will shape what partners like Hexagon build next, which will shape the broader trajectory of humanoid robots in European industry.

## What Comes After April

The April test deployment is planned to ensure full integration for the actual pilot phase. The pilot phase is due to start in summer 2026.

Given the pace at which manufacturing robotics has advanced over the past 24 months, the distance from two robots in Leipzig to two hundred robots across Europe may be shorter than the history of industrial automation would suggest.

Europe's factories are watching. The calculation has begun.
