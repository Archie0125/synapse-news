Three weeks of testimony, featuring a "jackass" exchange with Musk, a LinkedIn co-founder's name-drop and a gag trophy, came to an end on Wednesday in a federal courtroom in Oakland. Closing arguments are scheduled for Thursday morning.

The case is Musk v. OpenAI. The stakes are high: Musk has sued Altman, Greg Brockman and Microsoft, and is pushing for the removal of Altman and Brockman. OpenAI has framed the lawsuit as driven by jealousy, saying Musk left the company in 2018 after failing to gain control.

## What the Jury Heard

The testimony phase of the trial produced a remarkable public record of OpenAI's early years and the breakdown of its relationship with Musk.

Altman took the stand on Tuesday, May 12, rejecting Musk's claim that he betrayed OpenAI's original vision. According to Altman, Musk knew of plans for a for-profit enterprise and sought a majority stake. "An early number that Mr Musk threw out was that he should have 90 percent of the equity to start," Altman told the jury. Musk left OpenAI's board in 2018 to pursue his own AI development.

Musk's lawyer, Steven Molo, sought to undermine Altman's reliability, asking: "Have you misled people when you do business?"" Altman replied: "I do not think so." Musk claims the entire founding ethos of the organization was premised on one.

The final day of testimony yielded a striking anecdote. In an effort to prove OpenAI's chief futurist Joshua Achiam's account of a heated exchange with Musk, OpenAI's lawyers brought a small golden trophy to court that he said he received afterward. Separately, LinkedIn co-founder Reid Hoffman was name-dropped during testimony.

## The Legal Core

Musk claims OpenAI violated its original mission to operate as a nonprofit focused on developing artificial intelligence for the benefit of humanity.

OpenAI has pushed back on the claims. Third, that Musk's lawsuit is not driven by genuine concern about charitable mission but by competitive animus: xAI, his own AI company, competes directly with OpenAI.

## The Microsoft Angle

Among the notable testimony was that of Microsoft executives, including CEO Satya Nadella and technology chief Kevin Scott. Discovery showed Nadella worried as early as April 2022 about OpenAI supplanting Microsoft, even as the company invested heavily in the organization.

Nadella testified that Microsoft let go of some rights early on "so that OpenAI could continue to scale and thrive." It suggested that even Microsoft, OpenAI's largest backer, understood that the nonprofit structure was load-bearing — that it was not merely a legal formality but a signal to the research community and donors that OpenAI's incentives were different from those of a conventional startup. Whether that understanding constitutes a legal commitment is precisely what the jury must decide.

## What a Verdict Could Mean

A ruling for Musk would send shockwaves through Silicon Valley. It would potentially unwind one of the most valuable corporate structures ever created and establish a precedent that nonprofit origins can constrain for-profit transformations decades into the future. Practically, it would raise existential questions about OpenAI's ability to continue operating and raising capital.

A ruling for OpenAI would validate the de facto model by which AI companies have navigated the tension between public-interest framing and private capital: make aspirational nonprofit commitments early, restructure when the economics require it, and defend the transformation as necessary rather than opportunistic.

Neither outcome is tidy.

Jury deliberations are expected to begin Thursday, following closing arguments. The AI industry, which has watched every day of testimony with unusual intensity, is waiting.
