Quantum computing has a credibility problem. A field that has promised transformational hardware for two decades has produced a string of impressive announcements that rarely survive close scrutiny from independent physicists. When Microsoft took the stage at Build 2026 in San Francisco on June 2 to unveil Majorana 2—its second-generation topological quantum chip—it brought new qubit performance numbers and a familiar cloud of scientific skepticism.

The headline claim is dramatic: Majorana 2 achieves qubits 1,000 times more reliable than its predecessor, with a mean qubit lifetime of 20 seconds and some instances lasting as long as one minute. According to Microsoft, the original Majorana 1 chip achieved qubit lifetimes of five to 10 seconds, though Science News reports the previous parity lifetime was measured in milliseconds. Microsoft also says the chip brings its timeline to a commercially viable, scalable quantum computer forward to 2029—cutting its previous projection by roughly half.

## What Topological Qubits Are Supposed to Do

To understand why Microsoft's Majorana approach is both theoretically compelling and empirically contested, it helps to understand what makes qubits hard to keep alive in the first place.

Microsoft's approach proposes a different architecture. Topological qubits store information not in a single physical location but distributed across exotic quasiparticles called Majorana fermions. The key insight is that quantum information encoded in parity—the evenness or oddness of electrons in a wire—is inherently less sensitive to local disturbances. In theory, because the information is spread across the wire rather than held in a single particle, the encoded state is more resistant to local disturbances.

This "hardware-protected" approach, if it works, would reduce the overhead for error correction dramatically and potentially allow a smaller number of physical qubits to achieve the error rates needed for practical computation.

## The Materials Upgrade Behind the Numbers

Majorana 2's performance improvement relative to its predecessor stems primarily from a change in the superconducting material used in the device. The first generation used aluminum superconductors; Majorana 2 switches to lead. Lead's properties appear to provide better shielding against the external disturbances that cause qubit errors, and Microsoft's team used an agentic AI system called Microsoft Discovery to help manage manufacturing of the new device.

The role of AI in the chip's development is itself notable. Microsoft Discovery's agents automated complex measurements that took weeks when done by a person, analyzed nearly two decades of data across multiple formats, and helped find an uncalibrated temperature sensor reading in raw fabrication data. "The agents can really accelerate things as much or as little as you want," said Chetan Nayak, a Microsoft technical fellow. "It can be as little as pulling information together and summarizing it, or it can go further down the road of synthesizing it more or generating an interesting hypothesis."

Qubit dimensions remain extremely small—on the order of one hundredth of a millimeter—and operational speeds have been reduced to microseconds. Microsoft says these characteristics, together with the chip's reliability, put the team on a path to a scalable quantum computer.

## Where the Science Gets Complicated

Microsoft's claims met a familiar response from the physics community: cautious skepticism rooted in a field with a troubled history.

Physicist Henry Legg of the University of St Andrews was direct in his assessment. "Nothing in this preprint resolves the fundamental issues" with topological quantum computing, he said, pointing to a critical methodological gap in Microsoft's published data. Demonstrating a functional qubit requires two types of measurements—X and Z—but the new research presents only Z measurements. "Nothing in the presented data proves the existence of a topological qubit or Majoranas in these devices," Legg wrote.

The core scientific question is whether Microsoft has actually created Majorana fermions—the exotic particles the entire approach depends on. Many physicists in the field remain unconvinced that the company has produced the real thing rather than mimicking Majorana-like signatures with conventional physics. That history makes independent verification of any new Majorana claim particularly important.

Microsoft has not yet submitted the Majorana 2 results to peer review.

## The Stakes for Microsoft's Quantum Strategy

For Microsoft, Majorana 2 represents an unusually long bet. Microsoft placed its bet on an approach that requires first proving the existence of a new type of quasiparticle.

If Majorana 2's performance numbers are validated by independent researchers and the 2029 timeline for a commercially viable machine holds, Microsoft would have a structural advantage in quantum hardware: fewer physical qubits required, inherently lower error rates, and a platform that scales more gracefully than competing architectures. Microsoft has said such a machine could tackle intractable problems in global health, food supply, sustainability and energy production.

If the fundamental physics doesn't hold up under scrutiny, Microsoft faces the prospect of having invested more than a decade in an approach that competes on the wrong terrain.

Constellation Research analyst Holger Mueller said Microsoft has taken a "massive step forward" in the race to build quantum computers. Whether it is the breakthrough the company insists it is will depend not on what Microsoft says at Build 2026, but on what happens when physicists outside Redmond examine the devices themselves.
