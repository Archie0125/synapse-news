The numbers coming out of Crunchbase's H1 2026 venture capital analysis aren't just records — they're a category shift. Global startup investment reached **$510 billion in the first six months of 2026**, surpassing the $440 billion invested in all of 2025 and topping the previous half-year peak of $375 billion. Q1 alone generated $305 billion — the largest single quarter in venture history. Q2 added $205 billion, good for second place on that same all-time list.

The driver is singular and overwhelming: artificial intelligence. More than 70% of global startup capital in Q2 2026 targeted AI companies.

## The OpenAI-Anthropic Gravitational Pull

OpenAI and Anthropic together claimed **43% of all H1 2026 venture funding globally**. That figure deserves to be read twice. Two companies captured 43% of all the startup investment in the world for the half.

The defining deal was Anthropic's **$65 billion raise at a $965 billion post-money valuation**, which accounted for close to a third of global Q2 venture funding. It reflects investor conviction that the frontier AI market will be winner-concentrated enough to justify valuations approaching $1 trillion. The round gives Anthropic capital to compete on training compute, inference infrastructure, and enterprise sales at a level that few companies outside the Big Tech universe have ever attempted.

OpenAI's record-setting $122 billion financing in Q1 also contributed to the concentration. Between the two companies, the concentration of AI capital is extraordinary — and it's creating a bifurcated venture market where frontier AI labs receive once-unimaginable check sizes while the rest of the startup ecosystem competes for a substantially smaller pool.

## North America Dominates

North American companies captured **$392 billion in H1 2026** — the vast majority of the global total. North American investment was the largest on record in Q1, while Q2 investment totaled $137.2 billion, the second-highest on record. The United States captured roughly 83% of global Q1 funding before settling back to about two-thirds in Q2, in line with proportions in Q2 2025.

The regional distribution tells an important story about the AI investment geography. The U.S. dominance in frontier AI capital is not evenly spread across the startup economy. It is concentrated in a small number of very large companies, while deal count remains well below prior highs.

In North America specifically, Crunchbase notes that deal count remained well below prior high marks for recent years — meaning historically high investment came from giant rounds, not more deals. This is the defining characteristic of the current funding environment: a narrow pipeline of massive AI bets alongside a more constrained environment for earlier-stage companies.

## The Exit Market Opens

H1 2026 also delivered the most significant exit activity in years. Q2 alone saw **32 companies complete IPOs at billion-dollar-plus valuations** and **24 companies acquired at $1 billion or more**, generating $113 billion in M&A transaction value.

The landmark exits include:
- **SpaceX IPO**: Completed in June 2026 at a $1.77 trillion valuation, raising $75 billion — the largest IPO in history by a significant margin. SpaceX later confirmed its intent to acquire Anysphere (Cursor) for $60 billion.
- **Cursor acquisition by SpaceX**: At $60 billion, this is the largest startup M&A deal ever recorded. SpaceX confirmed its intent to acquire Anysphere less than a week after the IPO, having first announced an option to purchase the company in April.
**Cerebras IPO**: The AI chip design company raised $5.6 billion in its May IPO.

The return of a functioning exit market matters for the venture ecosystem as a whole. The opening of that window in 2026 is a structural catalyst for the next cycle of early-stage investment.

## The Stage Distribution Problem

Behind the record headline numbers lies a more complicated picture for early-stage founders. Late-stage venture funding totaled $134 billion in Q2, down from Q1 but up 141% from Q2 2025. Early-stage funding rose more than 100% year-over-year to reach $58.9 billion across the quarter. Global seed funding totaled $12 billion in Q2, with a widening gap between a handful of exceptionally large financings and traditional seed rounds.

This pattern reflects a fundamental tension in the current venture market. Frontier AI companies reaching late-stage rounds are raising at valuations that were previously reserved for public companies, and the concentration of institutional capital chasing a small number of likely winners has pushed check sizes to historic highs.

The implication for founders earlier in the journey is asymmetric access. Getting from zero to seed has not fundamentally changed.

## Non-AI Sectors: Defense and Healthcare Hold

Not all of the H1 2026 momentum was AI-driven. Defense technology and healthcare saw significant rounds as well. **Anduril Industries** raised a $5 billion Series H in May, led by Thrive Capital and Andreessen Horowitz. **Prometheus**, a physical AI startup that counts Jeff Bezos as a co-founder, raised $12 billion in an early-stage financing.

These rounds suggest that while frontier AI labs capture the headlines and the bulk of the capital, the AI investment boom has a second layer: companies applying AI to high-value physical domains where automation can unlock meaningful efficiency gains or capability enhancements in sectors like defense, logistics, manufacturing, and drug discovery.

## What Comes Next

The H1 2026 record raises an obvious question: is this pace sustainable?

The bullish case is that the numbers make sense given the scale of the technology opportunity. The frontier AI market is competing for a share of global GDP at a moment when the technology's applicability to knowledge work is accelerating. Trillion-dollar valuations for companies that might capture a fraction of that TAM aren't obviously wrong even if they are uncomfortable.

The bearish case is that $510 billion in six months has created concentrated positions in a small number of companies whose ability to generate revenue proportional to their valuations remains unproven at the required scale. If any of the mega-rounds, such as Anthropic at $965 billion, fail to execute on the growth assumptions priced into their valuations, the unwinding could be severe.

What seems certain is that the structure of the venture market has permanently changed. Two AI labs accounted for 43% of global startup funding in H1. The largest IPO in history was a rocket, satellite and AI company that then moved to acquire the largest startup M&A target in history. The rules that governed venture capital for the past two decades are being rewritten, and the rewrite is happening faster than most participants can track.
