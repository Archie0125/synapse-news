For three years, "AI glasses" meant one thing in the consumer market: a pair of Ray-Ban frames with a camera and Meta's voice assistant embedded in the hinge. That changed on June 23, 2026, when Meta launched its first self-branded AI glasses line — the Meta Adventurer, the Meta Fury, and the Meta Glasses by Kylie — under its own name for the first time, without the Ray-Ban badge, and at a price point designed to remove the remaining barriers to mass adoption.

The launch is more significant than a product refresh. It represents Meta's first step in establishing a hardware brand independent of its licensing partnership with EssilorLuxottica, even as that partnership continues to produce the glasses themselves. And at $299, it sets a reference price for what a capable AI wearable costs — a figure that makes Snap's simultaneously launched AR Specs at $2,195 look like a niche professional device rather than a consumer product.

## What You Get for $299

The Meta Adventurer and Meta Fury carry identical hardware specifications to the Ray-Ban Meta Gen 2 that launched last year: a 12-megapixel camera capable of 3K video capture, a five-microphone array for noise-resistant voice activation, and a battery rated at eight hours of continuous use. The included charging case provides an additional 40 hours of capacity. The specs are essentially unchanged from the Ray-Ban generation — the differentiation is in the brand, the price, and the AI software.

The Adventurer takes a slimmer everyday silhouette that leans toward professional use. The Fury goes with a larger, more rectangular frame that skews athletic. A third option — Meta Glasses by Kylie, designed in collaboration with Kylie Jenner — offers a slim oval style at $399. Meta describes a total of 26 styles across the lineup, spanning different frame shapes, colors, and lens options including prescription.

What is genuinely new is the AI running on the device. Meta Glasses ship with Muse Spark, a proprietary Meta AI model built specifically for wearable inference — optimized to run efficiently within the thermal and battery constraints of a glasses form factor rather than streaming requests to a server on every interaction. Muse Spark is the same model that received an update on the Ray-Ban Meta Display glasses in late July, but its debut on the self-branded Meta Glasses line represents the first time Meta has shipped its own AI model as a primary feature rather than a branded experience built on top of general-purpose AI.

## Live Translation Across 20 Languages

The single feature most highlighted in Meta's launch communications is live translation, which with Muse Spark now covers 20 languages: English, Spanish, French, Italian, German, Portuguese, Arabic, Mandarin, Dutch, Finnish, Greek, Hindi, Indonesian, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Swedish, Thai, Turkish, and Vietnamese. That represents a net addition of 14 languages from the previous Ray-Ban Meta roster, with Mandarin, Korean, Japanese, Arabic, and Hindi being the most commercially significant additions for Asian and Middle Eastern markets.

The live translation capability uses the device's microphones to capture incoming speech, processes it on-device or with minimal cloud round-trips, and plays the translated output through the speakers built into the frame arms — enabling real-time conversation without pulling out a phone. Meta has not released latency figures, but reviews from the first wave of hands-on testers describe translation delays of roughly one to two seconds, which is usable for conversational exchange though not comfortable for rapid back-and-forth dialogue.

That lag will narrow as both the on-device model and the underlying hardware improve. The more meaningful strategic point is that Meta has established live translation as a table-stakes feature at the $299 price tier — a capability that required a $500-plus device or a dedicated handheld translator two years ago.

## The Brand Strategy Shift

The absence of Ray-Ban branding on the Meta Glasses line is not a licensing dispute or a supply chain decision. It is deliberate product strategy. Meta controls approximately 76% of the global smart glasses market by units shipped — 9.6 million units in the preceding year — and has reached a stage where it can begin building consumer recognition for its own hardware brand rather than borrowing it from a century-old eyewear label.

Ray-Ban's brand equity was essential for Meta's first entry into the category. Most consumer AI devices that preceded the Ray-Ban Meta partnership failed because they were worn either on faces people had never seen the shape of, or priced such that the hardware had to carry the entire purchase decision. Putting the product inside a Ray-Ban frame gave it immediate fashion legitimacy. Three years and several million units later, Meta's own AI glasses brand is sufficiently established to stand independently.

The EssilorLuxottica relationship continues. Ray-Ban Meta Gen 3 is expected to debut at Meta Connect in September 2026, with dual cameras and longer Live AI session support. But from June 2026 onward, Meta will have a parallel product line that extends its reach to consumers who prefer its own designs or who are being introduced to AI glasses for the first time through a Meta-branded product rather than an eyewear heritage brand.

## The Apple Shadow

The timing of Meta's self-branded launch is impossible to read without reference to Apple. Apple's N50 smart glasses project, which was broadly expected to debut at WWDC 2026, was delayed last month until WWDC 2027 amid persistent concerns about the product's privacy architecture and thermal performance at the target price point. The delay opens at least a 12-month window in which Meta, Samsung, Snap, and Qualcomm-backed partners will compete for the smart glasses category without facing Apple's distribution power or ecosystem lock-in.

Meta's $299 launch is, in part, a landgrab during that window. By establishing its own brand and its own price anchor before Apple enters the market, Meta is attempting to define what "normal" looks like for AI wearables — making Apple's inevitable first entry look either like a premium option or, if Apple matches the price, a validation of Meta's approach.

The dynamics are familiar from the smartphone era: the Android ecosystem established pricing norms and feature expectations that constrained Apple's positioning for years after the iPhone's launch. Meta is clearly attempting to apply the same logic in reverse for smart glasses. Whether it works depends on how much Apple's brand equity and ecosystem integration can override a year or more of Meta's category leadership — a question whose answer will not be known until late 2027 at the earliest.

## What the $299 Price Means for the Category

Snap's simultaneous launch of AR Specs at $2,195 illustrates the two markets that currently exist in smart glasses. Snap's product offers full environmental AR overlay — digital elements rendered on top of the physical world — and targets professionals and developers willing to pay a premium for early capabilities. Meta's Adventurer and Fury deliver camera, voice, and AI without environmental AR, and target the mass consumer market at a price point below many smartphone accessories.

The $299 tier is the market that matters for adoption scale. At that price, the purchase decision is comparable to a pair of AirPods Pro rather than a consumer electronics investment requiring justification. If Meta can sell millions of units at $299 — and its 76% market share and existing Ray-Ban Meta install base suggest it can — the compounding effect on AI glasses as a platform will be significant: more users generate more data to improve Muse Spark, more users justify more developer investment in glasses-native applications, and more users create social normalization that reduces the friction for subsequent buyers.

That feedback loop is exactly what Meta needs to maintain its lead in wearable AI as hardware incumbents like Apple, hardware challengers like Samsung, and software incumbents like Google all attempt to establish their own positions in the category before consumer habits solidify.
