Meta Platforms reported second-quarter results on Wednesday, July 29, and shares slid in extended trading. The company's Q2 2026 earnings, released after the close on July 29, showed revenue of $60.8 billion, a 28% year-over-year increase, with ad impressions up 14% and average price per ad up 12%. But an EPS miss of nearly 14% (against an LSEG consensus of $7.22) and weaker-than-expected Q3 revenue guidance sent shares lower in extended trading.

The result highlights a question for investors: what happens to margins when a company spends $31 billion on capital expenditures in a single quarter to build AI infrastructure?

## Where the Numbers Came In

Meta's top line was unambiguously strong. Ad impressions grew 14% year-over-year, average price per ad climbed 12%, and total revenue landed at $60.8 billion against Wall Street's $60.2 billion estimate—a modest beat on the top line that masked severe pressure below it.

Total costs and expenses surged 55% to $42.0 billion, a figure that includes legal charges and severance expenses. Net income fell to $15.8 billion and diluted earnings per share came in at $6.18, well short of the analyst consensus of $7.22 per share.

The miss broke down roughly like this: $2.4 billion in charges related to legal proceedings (the company did not specify which cases) and $1.2 billion in severance expenses connected to the approximately 8,000-employee layoff wave announced in May 2026. Excluding those items, CFO Susan Li said operating income would have increased 9% year over year. The results, along with a Q3 guidance midpoint below consensus, came as shares slid in extended trading.

## Capital Expenditure: The Number That Spooked the Street

A notable data point in the report was the update to Meta's full-year 2026 capital expenditure outlook. The company narrowed its guidance to $130 billion to $145 billion, from the $125 billion to $145 billion range given previously. The low end of the range rose by $5 billion.

In the just-completed Q2, Meta spent $31.1 billion on capital expenditures, including principal payments on finance leases.

But in the near term, the ramp-up in infrastructure spending is outpacing the revenue contribution from AI-native products.

## Zuckerberg's Pitch—and Its Limits

CEO Mark Zuckerberg said on the earnings call that AI is accelerating the core business, and that a significant portion of Meta's compute will go toward training models, growing the core business and delivering personal agents and new products. "AI is accelerating our core business today, powering our next generation of products, and opening the door to entirely new enterprise opportunities," he said.

Meta debuted its Muse Spark 1.1 model earlier in July, and Zuckerberg said the company is also looking to grow a large business serving large customers.

Wall Street's skepticism is not about the vision. It is about the time horizon and the free cash flow in the interim. Meta's free cash flow fell to $784 million from $8.55 billion a year earlier, and Q3 revenue guidance of $61 billion to $64 billion (midpoint $62.5 billion) came in below analysts' expectation of $63.15 billion.

## The Contrast With Microsoft

Meta's challenge is different. Its AI spend is primarily serving free consumer products through advertising monetization—a model where the AI-to-revenue chain has more intermediate links and longer latency. When the story is "AI will make our ads better," investors accept that framing at much lower capex levels. When capex guidance runs up to $145 billion and revenue guidance is below expectations, investors start asking whether the model can generate sufficient returns.

## What's Next

For now, Meta exits its Q2 with a contradictory profile: revenue up 28%, but costs up 55% and free cash flow sharply lower.
