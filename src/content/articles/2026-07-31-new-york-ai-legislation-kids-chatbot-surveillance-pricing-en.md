New York's state legislature closed its 2026 session on June 5 by passing a package of artificial intelligence bills. The package includes a ban on AI companion chatbots for minors, a prohibition on surveillance pricing, an AI training data transparency act, the FAIR News Act, and a one-year moratorium on permitting large data centers. Governor Kathy Hochul has until Dec. 31 to sign each bill, veto it, or allow it to become law unsigned.

The breadth of the New York package—spanning consumer protection, children's safety, market fairness, and media integrity—signals a new phase in US state AI regulation, one in which legislatures are moving from single-issue bills to comprehensive regulatory frameworks that address AI's societal impacts across multiple domains simultaneously.

## Kids Chatbot Ban: The Centerpiece

The most prominent measure is S 9051, which passed the Assembly 137-0 and the Senate 60-0. The bill prohibits AI companies from offering "companion" chatbots—products designed to simulate emotional or social relationships—to users under 18.

The law carries teeth. Violations are subject to fines of up to $25,000 per incident, with enforcement authority vested in the New York Attorney General. Companion chatbot operators may not offer certain unsafe features to a New York resident unless the operator can determine the user is not a minor.

The bill bars features such as suggesting the chatbot is human or alive, using deception about the chatbot's non-sentient nature, and engaging in flattery or sycophancy.

The legislation is partly a response to documented harms from AI companion products. New York's bill frames these products, when directed at minors, as categorically inappropriate regardless of content moderation.

## Surveillance Pricing Prohibition

The second major measure targets surveillance pricing—the practice of using personal data to set individualized prices.

The New York law prohibits retailers from accessing individualized pricing that uses personal data in this manner. It permits bona fide discounts and loyalty programs.

Businesses using automated pricing based on non-personal factors must disclose the system and explain what categories of inputs influence prices.

New York's legislature has chosen a side.

## AI Training Data Transparency Act

The training data transparency bill requires generative AI developers to publicly disclose information about the datasets used to develop their models. The disclosure requirement would apply when a model is released or substantially modified.

The disclosures include data sources, whether copyrighted or personal information was used, and how the data was processed, which goes further than California's similar 2024 law.

It creates a parallel disclosure regime: even if training on copyrighted works is found to be legal, companies would still need to tell consumers that they did so.

## FAIR News Act and AI-Labeling Requirements

The FAIR News Act would require transparency labeling for news media content composed or created through generative AI. The requirement applies to news content substantially composed or created through generative AI, which must be conspicuously labeled.

The labeling duty covers news media content that is published, broadcast, or otherwise disseminated.

## Five-Year Moratorium on AI-Powered Children's Toys

One report describes a five-year moratorium on AI-enabled children's toys while a task force studies their developmental impact.

## New York vs. California: A Regulatory Competition

The passage of New York's package creates a significant parallel AI regulatory framework to California's existing laws, though the two are focused on different risks.

New York's package focuses primarily on the consumer layer—what companies can do with AI systems in their products when those products reach consumers.

Together, they suggest a division of labor emerging in US state AI regulation: California regulating the creation and deployment of AI systems by their developers; New York regulating the consumer-facing applications of those systems by the companies that build products on top of them.

For companies operating at scale in both states—which includes virtually every major AI-enabled consumer technology company—compliance will require separate analysis of both frameworks, as their requirements often address different actors in the same supply chain.

## Looking Forward

The New York package's passage may encourage other states—particularly Massachusetts, Illinois, and Colorado, which have active AI regulatory agendas—to move similar measures. Federal preemption of state AI laws remains possible under proposed legislation, but as long as the federal landscape remains fragmented, state-by-state compliance will remain the reality for AI companies operating in the US market.
