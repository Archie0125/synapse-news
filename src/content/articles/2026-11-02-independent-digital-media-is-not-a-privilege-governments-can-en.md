On November 11, Lina Attalah, founder and editor-in-chief of the independent Egyptian news outlet [Mada Masr](https://www.madamasr.com/en/), will face trial for the second time in just two weeks. This time, she faces charges of spreading false news, an accusation that has become one of the Egyptian government's most frequently used tools for prosecuting journalists.

The trial follows a conviction on October 27, when Attalah was [found guilty](https://www.madamasr.com/en/2026/10/27/news/u/lina-attalah-in-custody-after-court-issues-le3-mn-fine-orders-mada-masrs-closure/) of managing an unlicensed news website. The Mada Masr team had repeatedly applied for a license, but their attempts went unacknowledged. The proceedings took place in a single hearing, which Mada Masr [states](https://www.instagram.com/p/DfM44U5m32q/?mdxt=MXI2aWJiaTE5YzJ5bg==) included serious violations of her right to a fair trial. She was sentenced to the maximum fine of 3 million Egyptian pounds (approximately 55,000 USD), forcibly removed from the courtroom, and held for seven hours until the fine was paid. The court also ordered Mada Masr's closure and the confiscation of all equipment belonging to the organization.

The current wave of repression has also targeted [Matsada2sh](https://matsda2sh.com/en), a fact-checking and open-source intelligence initiative. Its entire six-person newsroom was [violently arrested](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/04/egyptian-journalist-faces-terrorism-charges-entire-newsroom-detained-matsadaash-press-freedom), illegally detained, and interrogated under torture before being charged with terrorism-related offenses and spreading false news. The team remains in pretrial detention.

According to CPJ, at least [25 other journalists](https://cpj.org/data/imprisoned/currently-imprisoned/?status=Imprisoned&cc_fips[]=EG&group_by=location¤tly-imprisoned=1) are currently incarcerated in Egypt.

Governments seeking to control the flow of information increasingly rely on a combination of legal harassment, financial pressure, criminal prosecution, and restrictions on the infrastructure that makes independent publishing possible. Egypt's media licensing regime is a particularly troubling example. Requiring news organizations to obtain government authorization to operate creates an obvious mechanism for restricting who gets to publish and whose voices are allowed to reach the public.

The charge of spreading false news is similarly dangerous. Governments around the world have increasingly invoked the language of misinformation to justify restrictions on speech, often without meaningful safeguards against political abuse, as we laid out in our [recent submission](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/04/effs-submission-un-ohchr-protection-human-rights-defenders-digital-age) to the United Nations Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights. Governments should never be the arbiters of which reporting is permissible, particularly when that reporting concerns their own conduct.

### **Why Mada Masr matters**

Founded in 2013, Mada Masr has spent more than a decade doing the kind of journalism that authoritarian governments find most threatening: documenting abuses, amplifying marginalized voices, and reporting on issues that powerful institutions would prefer remain hidden.

Its reporting has given torture survivors and the families of political prisoners a platform. It has opened space for debate about reproductive rights. It has brought frontline reporting from Sudan and Gaza, and stood alongside refugees facing xenophobic attacks and security crackdowns.

Emerging as a digital-first publication, in a media environment where traditional outlets were increasingly constrained, being purely digital allowed Mada Masr’s founders to build an independent publication outside the structures of legacy media.

At a time when [only 43% of Egyptians had access to the internet](https://english.ahram.org.eg/News/84996.aspx), going fully digital meant taking a big risk, but it was one that has been rewarding: Despite being blocked by the Egyptian government since 2017, Mada Masr has continued to reach Egyptian and global audiences, and Attalah has received numerous accolades, including the [Knight International Journalism Award](https://www.icfj.org/news/fareed-zakaria-and-two-international-digital-news-pioneers-receive-prestigious-journalism), the [Ordre des Arts et des Lettres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordre_des_Arts_et_des_Lettres), and a spot in [TIME Magazine’s 100 most influential people](https://time.com/collections/100-most-influential-people-2020/5888226/lina-attalah/) (2020).

Mada Masr has also consistently engaged with technology and advocated for a free and independent internet for the global majority. It has covered dozens of hot-button tech stories in recent years, from [Egypt’s Cybercrime Bill](https://www.madamasr.com/en/2018/04/16/feature/politics/cybercrime-bill-nears-committee-approval-with-harsher-penalties-for-site-admins-protection-for-bona-fides/) and [Israel’s use of AI weaponry in Gaza](https://www.madamasr.com/2024/04/05/news/u/%D8%A5%D8%B3%D8%B1%D8%A7%D8%A6%D9%8A%D9%84-%D8%AA%D8%B3%D8%AA%D8%AE%D8%AF%D9%85-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%B0%D9%83%D8%A7%D8%A1-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A7%D8%B5%D8%B7%D9%86%D8%A7%D8%B9%D9%8A-%D9%84%D9%82/), to stories about the “[privacy chaos](https://www.madamasr.com/en/2026/08/12/feature/society/privacy-chaos-from-fraudulent-sim-registration-to-biometric-data-collection/)” caused by biometrics to more [personal essays](https://www.madamasr.com/2019/09/06/opinion/u/%D8%B9%D9%85%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%82%D8%A9%D9%8B-%D8%A8%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%8A%D9%86%D9%8E-%D9%85%D9%88%D8%A7%D8%B7%D9%86-%D8%AC%D8%AF%D9%8A%D8%AF-%D8%A2%D8%AA%D9%8D-%D9%84%D9%84%D9%81%D8%B6%D8%A7%D8%A1-%D8%A7/) about the importance of the internet and tech. In August, they launched an [open call for proposals](https://www.madamasr.com/en/2026/08/09/feature/society/open-call-for-proposals-and-reporting-practices-on-artificial-intelligence/) on original critical reporting on artificial intelligence.

Mada Masr may be thoroughly Egyptian, but it doesn’t belong just to Egypt. Its team has made into a publication that represents the kind of independent, local, grounded journalism that international audiences need, particularly at a time when international reporting on state repression is lacking. They built on-the-ground networks to center local voices in Sudan (publishing in both [English](https://www.madamasr.com/en/topic/2023-sudan-war/) and [Arabic](https://www.madamasr.com/topic/%d9%86%d8%b4%d8%b1%d8%a9-%d8%a7%d9%84%d8%b3%d9%88%d8%af%d8%a7%d9%86/) on the conflict) and Gaza out of a sense of cross-border responsibility and solidarity.

This is precisely why its potential closure should concern everyone who cares about a free press. The ability to publish independently, investigate powerful institutions, and to share information without government interference is fundamental to the exercise of free expression.

### **From solidarity to action**

The international community has repeatedly expressed concern about restrictions on [press](https://rsf.org/en/country/egypt) and [internet](https://freedomhouse.org/country/egypt/freedom-net/2022) freedom in Egypt. More than 100 organizations have now joined [statements](https://timep.org/2026/10/23/urgent-global-call-following-the-referral-of-mada-masr-editor-in-chief-lina-attalah-to-trial/) of [solidarity](https://timep.org/2026/10/28/organizations-condemn-mada-masr-shutdown-stand-with-editor-in-chief-lina-attalah/) with Mada Masr, and with [Matsada2sh](https://timep.org/2026/09/30/global-call-for-the-release-of-entire-newsroom-at-egyptian-investigative-journalism-platform-matsadaash/).

These expressions of support matter, particularly when journalists are increasingly isolated and under threat—but solidarity must also translate into sustained political and institutional pressure.

The international governments and institutions that maintain relationships with Egypt—including those in the European Union, the United Kingdom, and the United States—should make clear that the prosecution of journalists and the forced closure of independent media outlets are unacceptable.

The cases against Lina Attalah, Mada Masr, and Matsada2sh must be dropped. The journalists who remain in detention must be released. And Egypt's licensing regime, with its profound chilling effects on access to information and independent knowledge production, must be brought to an end.

There is a particular urgency to defending independent media at a moment when governments are finding ever more ways to restrict the circulation of information. Whether through internet shutdowns, platform restrictions, surveillance, or the criminalization of reporting, the result is often the same: fewer opportunities for people to understand what is happening around them, to challenge official narratives, and to hold power accountable.

We cannot allow the closure of one of Egypt's most important independent newsrooms and the prosecution of its editor-in-chief to become another routine episode in the global erosion of press freedom. Nor should we wait until the last independent journalists have been silenced before recognizing what has been lost.
