The government’s surveillance powers grew rapidly after Sept. 11, 2001, some in public view and some in secret. EFF suspected but couldn’t prove that the National Security Agency was spying on Americans' communications. The New York Times sat on a story about it for over a year, finally publishing it in December 2005. Then, in January 2006, a retired AT&T technician named [Mark Klein](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/03/memoriam-mark-klein-att-whistleblower-about-nsa-mass-spying) walked into EFF's office with a manila folder of documents that blew the story wide open.

Welcome to the third episode of “Privacy’s Defenders,” a podcast about the people – lawyers, journalists, hackers, and others – who’ve fought to secure your digital liberties since before most people even knew what the internet was.

(You can also find this episode on [YouTube](https://youtu.be/EvzMt1ap9sU?si=RPrmwx4HIYykbiNi).)

In this episode, former EFF Executive Director Cindy Cohn talks with former EFF lawyer [Kevin Bankston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Bankston), who was at his desk drafting a lawsuit against AT&T when Klein showed up. Less than two weeks later, EFF sued the phone company on behalf of its own customers in [*Hepting v. AT&T*](https://www.eff.org/cases/hepting). Cindy speaks again with now-retired Justice Department lawyer Tony Coppolino, who asked for the opportunity to defend the case. He called EFF with a warning after Klein’s documents were filed in court: If they were classified, EFF would be in big trouble. You’ll also hear from Klein himself, and from Carolyn Jewel, a romance novelist and AT&T customer who became the lead plaintiff in [EFF's related case against the NSA](https://www.eff.org/cases/jewel).

It's the story of a secret room in a San Francisco phone company building, a heroic whistleblower, and the people who refused to let mass surveillance stay hidden.

The “Privacy’s Defenders” podcast is a follow-up to Cindy’s book, “Privacy’s Defender: My Thirty-Year Fight Against Digital Surveillance,” bringing to life pivotal moments in the voices of those who fought for your rights. ## Summary

**TLDR:** NSA mass surveillance came to light because one technician walked in with a manila folder. That's a reminder that oversight still depends on individual courage, not on institutions.

- The third episode of EFF's 'Privacy's Defenders' podcast revisits AT&T technician Mark Klein, who brought evidence of NSA spying to EFF in January 2006.
- Klein's documents described a secret room in an AT&T building in San Francisco and led EFF to file Hepting v. AT&T less than two weeks later.
- The New York Times held its NSA story for over a year before publishing it in December 2005.
- The episode features Cindy Cohn, Kevin Bankston, former DOJ lawyer Tony Coppolino and Jewel v. NSA lead plaintiff Carolyn Jewel.
- Two decades later, the same pattern holds: the public often learns about surveillance programs through leaks and lawsuits, not official disclosure.
