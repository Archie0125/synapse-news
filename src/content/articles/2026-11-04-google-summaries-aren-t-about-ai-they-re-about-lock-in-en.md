Whether they call it AI summaries, knowledge panels, or AMP, Google takes actions that are less about functionality and more about locking you into its own ecosystem. Knowing that makes it possible for all of us to choose better services than Google's, defeating their anticompetitive goal. In this case, as it is so often, knowledge is power.

In the old days, when dinosaurs roamed the earth and getting online involved your computer making eldritch shrieking, Google was a website that took whatever you typed in and gave you a list of links that best matched what you were searching for. As it's grown beyond that, however, the core function of Google Search has moved from giving you the best answer to your query to a number of other things, most of them about making money for Google.

This is obviously not a new observation. It's at the core of what EFF Special Advisor Cory Doctorow named "[enshittification](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2024/06/podcast-episode-fighting-enshittification)." But it's important to remember that whatever shiny new name or technology is wrapped around these tools, it's not about the wrapping, it's about the monetization.

In the case of Google Search, the company has a history of rolling out new "features" that are less helpful to the searcher and more designed to keep you and your data within the Google ecosystem. One easy example of enshittification is putting sponsored links and ads at the top of search results, forcing you to scroll down past a bunch of not relevant links to get to your answers. Or, in some cases, accidentally clicking on sponsored links that might be designed to give you the opposite information of what you're looking for or for a competitor to the product you intended to buy.

The other clumsy version of this is Google's self-preferencing, where Google's own services—say YouTube if you're looking for a video—are weighted so that they appear at the top of a search over a competitor's. Similar to this was Google's push for websites, particularly news ones, to be made available via what it called ["Accelerated Mobile Pages" or AMP](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2020/07/googles-amp-canonical-web-and-importance-web-standards-0), which in theory stripped out features that might make loading a page on mobile too slow, but also handed over control of how webpages looked on mobile to Google and turned mobile links, often by default, into Google links.

But the other way that Google has historically kept your—and your valuable eyeballs—on its website has been to just obviate the need to even click on a link and leave Google at all. The first version of this was what Google calls "knowledge panels." These are snippets of information shown on the results page of a search that purport to answer your question. Knowledge panels predate the AI push and haven't always been what you could call [accurate](https://gizmodo.com/googles-algorithm-is-lying-to-you-about-onions-and-blam-1793057789).

Remember that it is Google's monetary incentives that drive the decisions and not necessarily the tech itself.

Knowledge panels are extremely similar to the AI summaries we now see at the top of Google Search results, except that instead of pulling quotes from specific linked sources, it's the AI doing the summarizing, though also sometimes with links. They are basically the same thing, mostly changed only in branding, because AI is now the cool kid on the block. The goal is essentially the same—it's less about providing you with the best answer to your query and more about making you think it's unnecessary to click a link and leave Google's ecosystem. And, more importantly, it keeps you on Google's pages longer to read the content. More time on the page is more time exposed to ads and sponsored links and more money for Google.

The biggest irony here is that *it didn’t even work*. People choosing to use “AI mode”—and possibly made accustomed to getting information as text instead of links through the knowledge panels and the AI summaries at the top—has caused [“lower monetization”](https://www.ft.com/content/eb386156-5bf9-4edd-80f2-803fdee1e33d?syn-25a6b1a6=1) via ads. Ads, remember, are Google’s main source of revenue. Like newspapers before them, Google is finding that counting on a monopoly on ad space maybe isn’t the most sound strategy in a shifting technological landscape. And to get out of the hole it has made for itself, it is lighting cash on fire to try to get back on top. Perhaps they should consider pivoting to video.

Every time you see a feature like this, no matter what it is called or what "new" technology they claim fuels it, remember that it is Google's monetary incentives that drive the decisions and not necessarily the tech itself. And you can always choose to ignore it, scroll past, disable it, or even [go to another service entirely](https://www.eff.org/pages/i-want-opt-out-software-made-big-tech).

## Summary

**TLDR:** EFF argues that Google's AI summaries continue the AMP and knowledge-panel playbook: keep users inside Google. Calling it AI doesn't change that business logic.

- EFF argues that AI summaries, knowledge panels and AMP share one goal: keeping users and their data inside Google's ecosystem.
- Search has shifted from returning the best links to monetization, which fits Cory Doctorow's idea of 'enshittification'.
- Sponsored results at the top and self-preferencing, such as pushing YouTube for video searches, are older examples of the same pattern.
- EFF's prescription is user awareness: once people see the lock-in strategy, they can choose alternative services.
- For publishers, AI answers that keep users on Google further reduce click-through traffic, the web's original deal with search engines.

## 摘要

**重點摘要：** EFF 認為，Google 的 AI 摘要延續的是 AMP 和知識面板那套打法：把使用者留在 Google 裡。冠上 AI 之名，商業邏輯還是一樣。

- EFF 認為，AI 摘要、知識面板和 AMP 都只有一個目的：把使用者和他們的資料留在 Google 生態系裡。
- Google 搜尋的核心已從提供最好的連結轉向變現，正符合 Cory Doctorow 提出的「平台劣化」（enshittification）概念。
- 把贊助連結放在最上面、優先推自家服務（例如搜尋影片時優先顯示 YouTube），都是同一套模式的早期例子。
- EFF 開出的解方是提高使用者意識：看清這套鎖定策略後，大家就能改用其他服務。
- 對內容發布者來說，把使用者留在 Google 頁面上的 AI 答案，會進一步減少點擊導流，而這原本是網站和搜尋引擎之間的基本交易。
