Texas is the [fastest-growing](https://www.houstonchronicle.com/projects/2026/tx-data-centers-map/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) data center market in the US and ground zero for Big Tech's largest AI buildouts. But the blue wave that swept the Lone Star State may jeopardize that position.

Democrat James Talarico won the US Senate seat against Ken Paxton. Gina Hinojosa won the race for governor against the Republican incumbent, Greg Abbott. At the state level, Democrats will take control of the Texas House for the first time in 24 years. Talarico and Hinojosa made data centers a central issue, discussing them constantly on the campaign trail.

Even before the polls closed, insiders in the data center industry noticed that the times were changing. At the industry's DCD conference in Ashburn, Virginia, on Tuesday, panelists lamented that Texas had been the easiest place to build for years—until September. That's when Abbott, perhaps seeing the increasingly [bipartisan distaste for these facilities](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/01/14/1131253/data-centers-are-amazing-everyone-hates-them/), began changing course himself.

Developers said the governor’s office had [stopped responding](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/30102026/texas-data-center-pushback/) to their emails. On September 21, Abbott [directed](https://gov.texas.gov/news/post/governor-abbott-directs-tceq-to-halt-data-center-permits) a key state regulator to halt data center permits. Many panelists at the conference were expecting this to mean that existing projects might be slowed and new ones could be halted altogether.

The governor-elect indeed seems eager to do that. Hinojosa supported tax breaks for data centers in 2023 as a way to boost construction jobs, but during her campaign she began pushing for a full-fledged moratorium.

That would be a problem for the hyperscalers of Big Tech, which have ambitious plans for AI training facilities in the state. The scale of projects already underway in Texas is massive. The Stargate Frontier data center in Shackelford County is projected to serve 1,820 megawatts—about as much as the nation’s largest data center, SpaceX’s Colossus 2 in Tennessee, and more than twice as much as the largest data center currently operating in Texas. Nexus Hubbard, also under construction and intended mostly to serve Anthropic, has a planned size of 6,400 megawatts and is the second-largest data center being built in the US, behind only Meta’s Hyperion project in Louisiana.

The potential for Democrats to order delays to these projects or moratoriums on new ones adds to growing anxiety that the AI buildout could stall. Banks and lenders are already getting [pickier](https://www.wsj.com/finance/the-new-bottleneck-in-americas-ai-build-out-a-shortage-of-cheap-money-0c14c84d?mod=tech_feat1_ai_pos5) about the projects they back. And given the extent to which the data center buildout is underpinning growth in the economy right now, those forces combined could have ripple effects.

Voters around the country, though, were [unambiguous](https://ballotpedia.org/Data_center-related_ballot_measures,_2026) in the midterms, sending a similar message on everything from state ballot questions to national offices. Voters supported efforts to protect ratepayers from increases in their electricity bills caused by data centers, and they favored some larger bans on new data centers altogether. If we needed any more convincing that Big Tech has fumbled its effort to sell voters on the idea that AI-powered abundance will make health care better, goods cheaper, and scientific breakthroughs more common, this was it.

A key question is whether the Texas counties with the largest data center presence were part of the reason these Democrats got elected, or if the anti-data-center vote came from elsewhere.

It’s complicated: The counties where much of the data center buildout is happening are around Dallas. Dallas County and others closest to it predictably went blue, but nearby Ellis County, which [leads](https://www.houstonchronicle.com/business/energy/article/ercot-grid-data-centers-22286592.php) in permitting requests for data centers, went for Greg Abbott and Ken Paxton. Cameron County, near the state’s southern border, is a hot spot for data centers and voted blue in the midterms, which was a shift. But Mitchell County and Scurry County, where a bunch of projects in west Texas are located, went red.

This is a big deal, because no single office in the state decides whether a project goes ahead or is halted. Decisions often reflect an interplay between city governments, county officials, and state offices.

Local officials who have some say over data center development may resist sweeping restrictions from the state. And Democrats pushing for a moratorium might also face pushback from the construction unions that supported some of their campaigns. So while the blue wave spells a death knell for Texas data centers in theory, election winners may find that following through on their opposition once in office is much more complicated than campaigning on it.

## Summary

**TLDR:** Texas just showed that 'build anything, anywhere' AI infrastructure plans now have a political price, and voters are willing to make Big Tech pay it.

- Texas, the fastest-growing US data center market, elected Democrats James Talarico to the Senate over Ken Paxton and Gina Hinojosa as governor over Greg Abbott, and Democrats took the Texas House for the first time in 24 years.
- Both Talarico and Hinojosa made data centers a central campaign issue.
- Abbott had already reversed course, directing a key state regulator on September 21 to halt data center permits after developers said his office stopped answering them.
- Hinojosa backed data center tax breaks in 2023 but campaigned on a full moratorium.
- Hyperscalers with large Texas AI plans may face delays, forcing them to look for other states, and growing bipartisan pushback suggests fewer easy places remain.
