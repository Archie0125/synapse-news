Yesterday, along with the ACLU, EFF filed an [amicus brief](https://www.eff.org/document/united-states-v-spurlock-eff-aclu-amicus-brief) in the Ninth Circuit court of appeals in *United States v. Spurlock*, a case involving a “tower dump” warrant. These overbroad warrants require cellular providers to turn over location data about every phone in a given area at a given time. This is the first such case to be heard by a federal appeals court since the [US Supreme Court ruled this summer](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/06/victory-supreme-court-says-constitution-protects-peoples-location-data) that highly similar “geofence” warrants are searches under the meaning of the Fourth Amendment.

*Spurlock* involves an investigation into two murder victims discovered on the side of a rural California highway. Police also found a car belonging to one of the victims several miles away on the same highway. They quickly sought a tower dump warrant for an area covering this stretch of road, which eventually revealed location data on nearly 1,700 unique phones. Faced with a motion to suppress, the district court relied on a [Fifth Circuit court of appeals decision](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2024/08/federal-appeals-court-finds-geofence-warrants-are-categorically-unconstitutional) about geofence warrants to hold that tower dumps are similarly categorically unconstitutional “general warrants” that violate the Fourth Amendment. However, the court refused to suppress evidence on the basis of the “good faith doctrine.”

Our amicus brief on appeal makes several key arguments.

First, we point out that the Supreme Court’s decision in the geofence case *Chatrie v. United States* this summer only strengthens the arguments that tower dumps are unconstitutional general warrants. Tower dumps are a somewhat older technique than geofence warrants, but they are both “reverse location searches,” in which police “tap into an all-encompassing database, allowing them to undertake nearly perfect, retrospective surveillance of countless persons and places.” This is the sort of untargeted “general warrant” the framers of the Fourth Amendment wanted to prevent. As we write in the brief, “Searching all people in an area without probable cause to believe they were all involved in the criminal activity under investigation reproduces the evils of general warrants and is not constitutionally permitted.” It’s this rule of law that prevents police from searching the houses of a hundred, or even a thousand people in the vicinity of a crime.

Second, we note that even if high courts continue to allow tower dump and geofence warrants in any cases, core Fourth Amendment law requires strict limits to protect the privacy of innocent bystanders, including limits on the scope and duration of the warrants, as well notice to affected individuals and after-the-fact deletion and minimization of irrelevant data.

Finally, we argue that the Fourth Amendment requires the court to set aside the good faith doctrine in cases like these and actually decide the constitutional issue. Too often courts pass the buck in cases involving novel surveillance techniques, leading to situations where police can continue using the technique for years or even decades without a definitive ruling.

## Summary

**TLDR:** Once the Supreme Court called geofence warrants searches, tower dumps that sweep up 1,700 phones to find one suspect became hard to defend. The Ninth Circuit now has to say so.

- EFF and the ACLU filed an amicus brief in United States v. Spurlock, asking the Ninth Circuit to hold that tower dump warrants are categorically unconstitutional.
- It is the first federal appeals case on tower dumps since the Supreme Court held in Chatrie v. United States this summer that geofence warrants are Fourth Amendment searches.
- In a rural California murder investigation, a tower dump returned location data on nearly 1,700 unique phones.
- The district court found tower dumps to be unconstitutional general warrants but declined to suppress the evidence under the good-faith doctrine.
- The outcome could set a West Coast precedent for every 'reverse location search' that police use.

## 摘要

**重點摘要：** 最高法院認定地理圍欄令狀屬於搜索後，為了找一名嫌犯撈走 1,700 支手機資料的基地台傾印，已經很難自圓其說。現在輪到第九巡迴法院表態。

- EFF 與 ACLU 在 United States v. Spurlock 案中提交法庭之友意見書，請求第九巡迴上訴法院認定基地台傾印令狀一律違憲。
- 這是最高法院今年夏天在 Chatrie v. United States 案認定地理圍欄令狀屬於第四修正案所稱的搜索之後，第一件由聯邦上訴法院審理的基地台傾印案。
- 在加州鄉間一起謀殺案的調查中，一次基地台傾印就調出將近 1,700 支手機的位置資料。
- 地方法院認定基地台傾印屬於違憲的「概括令狀」，卻以「善意例外」為由不排除證據。
- 判決結果可能為警方使用的各種「反向位置搜索」，在美國西岸立下判例。
