For two years, the AI infrastructure story has been told as a supply chain problem. Not enough H100s, then not enough B200s. Not enough transformers, not enough turbines, not enough gigawatts. The midterms showed that the scarcest input is something no one can fab: local permission.

WIRED called it "the data center referendum." In the 2026 midterms, voters chose to restrict data centers in 39 of 40 ballot measures. Some early tallies put it at 40 of 41. The exact count doesn't change much. You almost never see a result that lopsided in American local politics. School bonds don't win like that, and neither do stadium deals or marijuana legalization. On a single question, voters in very different places gave the same answer.

The tempting conclusion is that Americans have turned against AI. That reading is wrong, and the industry will waste money if it believes it.

## This was a vote about the power bill

Look at what voters were actually reacting to. In PJM, the grid operator covering 13 states and D.C., including Northern Virginia's "Data Center Alley," the capacity auction for 2025/26 cleared at $269.92 per megawatt-day. The year before, it cleared at $28.92. That's roughly a ninefold jump, and PJM itself pointed to data center load growth as a main driver. Those costs reach residential bills. People in Ohio, Maryland, and Virginia didn't need a white paper to notice. They saw it on their statements.

So the data center has become the most visible symbol of a tradeoff voters never agreed to. A trillion-dollar company gets a tax abatement, a substation, and a water allocation. The neighbors get a higher bill, a constant hum, and maybe 50 permanent jobs. Opponents don't have to argue that ChatGPT is bad. They only have to ask why their bill went up so Microsoft could train a model.

That framing crosses party lines, which explains the 39-of-40 result. Rural conservatives object to farmland being paved over. Suburban liberals object to gas turbines. Both object to the bill. You rarely see a coalition this wide, and it is very hard to break.

## Big Tech's money didn't buy consent

The broader election makes the point sharper. Democrats flipped both the House and the Senate. WIRED reports that more than $400 million from Elon Musk's super PAC "largely came up empty," and AI-industry super PACs did no better. The industry spent two years getting close to the Trump administration through White House events, super PAC checks, and lighter AI regulation. It then found out that access in Washington doesn't produce votes in Loudoun County or Laramie.

The response so far is mostly image management. OpenAI hired Jessica Schumer, Senator Chuck Schumer's daughter, right before the election. It also said it would start calling its data centers "compute campuses." Public Citizen's J.B. Branch called this "PR washing," and he's right. Renaming a 1-gigawatt facility a "campus" doesn't change the capacity auction result. Voters weren't confused about what the buildings are. They were angry about who pays for them.

## What nobody is talking about: local resistance helps the incumbents

Here's the second-order effect most coverage misses. Local opposition doesn't slow everyone down equally. It squeezes out independent developers and leaves the field to the hyperscalers.

Consider Crusoe. It's a Denver developer last valued at $30.9 billion, and it just raised $3.9 billion in September. It also had to sell its unfinished Wyoming project to Google. That project was announced with Tallgrass and planned to scale to 10 gigawatts. Google, which Crusoe had failed to sign as a customer, reportedly raised concerns about costs and timelines and then took over the site. Google now plans to invest $17 billion there.

Read that as a market signal. When permitting is slow, opposition is organized, and power interconnection takes years, the winners are companies that can carry a stalled project on their balance sheet, pay for lawyers in three counties at once, and promise utilities they'll cover grid upgrades. Google, Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta can do that. A venture-backed developer working against a debt schedule mostly can't.

Every restrictive ballot measure therefore makes already-approved sites more valuable, and most of those belong to the hyperscalers. Local resistance has become a moat. This is the irony: voters who wanted to rein in Big Tech may have handed it more market power. Expect more deals like Crusoe's, where independents find sites and do early development, then sell to a hyperscaler once the political risk becomes real.

## The permitting bill could make this worse

Now the federal response. On November 9, the Senate votes on the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA), a 417-page permitting reform bill from the bipartisan "four corners" group that includes Shelley Moore Capito and Sheldon Whitehouse. On paper, it's overdue. U.S. permitting is slow, and the American Clean Power Association estimates the bill could unlock 350 gigawatts of new clean energy. The White House supports it, and the Bipartisan Policy Center calls it "a really good bipartisan compromise."

There's a problem, though. The bill narrows environmental review and limits communities' ability to stop projects in court. NRDC's Christy Goldfuss says that under this legislation, "data centers would not need to answer to the concerns of local communities."

Think about the timing. A week after voters gave the clearest anti-data-center result in modern local politics, a lame-duck Congress is voting on a bill that weakens the tools those voters use. Even if the policy is defensible, the politics are bad. Passing it now would turn a local zoning argument into a national story about Washington overruling voters for Big Tech. Midterm results are already rearranging Democratic priorities, and the incoming majority will have every reason to reopen the data center language.

My position: permitting reform is necessary, especially for transmission. Treating community consent as an obstacle to engineer around is a mistake. If you take away people's ability to say no, they will look for other ways to block projects: ballot initiatives, recalls, state legislation. That process is already under way.

## The bear case: does any of this matter?

The honest counterargument is that ballot measures are local, and the buildout is enormous. In 2025, the four largest hyperscalers committed well over $350 billion in combined capex. Forty local votes can't stop that much money. Capital will go to Texas, Wyoming, or Louisiana, or to the Gulf states and other places with no ballot initiatives. Crusoe still has a 900-megawatt campus for Microsoft in Abilene due in mid-2027. Jane Street reportedly signed a $13 billion cloud deal with it. Money keeps moving.

That's partly right. The industry won't stop. It will get more expensive, slower, and more geographically concentrated. Every move to a "friendlier" jurisdiction means longer transmission lines, more on-site gas generation, and fewer locations near the fiber and talent that hyperscalers prefer. Europe has already seen this. Amsterdam paused new data centers in 2019. Ireland's grid operator has effectively frozen new Dublin connections because data centers use more than a fifth of the country's electricity. And now protesters have stormed a Microsoft construction site in Amsterdam. The pattern is clear: the AI buildout doesn't stop, but it gets pushed around.

## What to Watch

1. **BAAJA passes the Senate, but the data center provisions become the fight of 2027.** I expect the bill to clear the Senate. The new Democratic House majority will then push to exclude or condition data center projects, probably by tying fast-track treatment to ratepayer protections.

2. **Large-load tariffs become standard.** Ohio regulators have already approved AEP's tariff requiring data centers to pay for most of their contracted capacity whether they use it or not. Expect at least 10 more states to adopt similar "pay for what you reserve" rules by the end of 2027. This is the policy that actually answers what voters were angry about.

3. **More independents sell to hyperscalers.** Crusoe and Wyoming is the template. Watch for at least two more independent developers selling major projects to Google, Microsoft, Amazon, or Meta within 12 months.

4. **Behind-the-meter gas grows.** Hyperscalers will avoid the public grid fight by building their own generation on site. That ends the electric-bill argument but creates an emissions one.

5. **2028 brings even more ballot measures.** Activists now have a playbook that wins about 97% of the time. Expect the number of data center measures to at least double next cycle, and expect candidates in both parties to run against "compute campuses" by name.

The AI industry spent two years treating land, chips, and power as the binding constraints. The constraint that just showed up is neighbors, and neighbors can vote.
