Most people are reading this month's AI selloff as the start of a bust. I think the market is doing something less dramatic and more lasting: it has stopped paying for growth stories and started asking how these companies get paid.

Look at what happened. OpenAI went out to raise $30 billion at a $1.4 trillion valuation. According to the WSJ, FT and CNBC, it has secured about $11 billion. A week before its deadline it cut the target to as much as $20 billion at $1.25 trillion. That is a $150 billion markdown on the headline number, taken by the most famous AI company in the world. In the same stretch, AI stocks lost roughly $5 trillion in market value in a week, and Alphabet had its worst day in about a decade, falling 12% and losing around $500 billion.

Meanwhile Anthropic, which is about to list, filed an S-1 amendment showing its quarter-over-quarter growth roughly cut in half. SaaStr's summary of the 20VC episode put it bluntly: growth went "from 12x to 2x."

Here is what nobody is putting together. **Token usage is still climbing, and Ramp's data shows enterprise AI spending going flat.** The industry has spent three years treating usage as a stand-in for revenue. Those two lines are now separating, and that gap explains everything else this month, from OpenAI's discounted round to the ads now showing up in your chatbot.

## Usage up, spend flat: the deflation trap

When usage rises and spending doesn't, the price per unit is falling. Every frontier lab is caught in this. Models get cheaper to serve, competitors cut prices, open-weight models set a floor, and enterprise buyers who signed exploratory contracts in 2024 now know exactly what they need. Customers get more tokens for the same budget, which is great for them. For a lab valued on the assumption that revenue tracks usage, it's a slow leak.

Gartner's October 21 forecast can look like it says the opposite. It projects GenAI model spending growing 117% in 2026 and total software spending rising 14.6% to $1.68 trillion in 2027. But Gartner's own analyst, John-David Lovelock, has said that a meaningful share of software growth is price increases: the same seats at higher prices, plus AI add-ons and usage meters. Gartner also notes that enterprises mostly buy simple AI features embedded in tools from vendors they already use. Put that next to Ramp and you get a consistent picture. Money is moving toward a few model providers and incumbent SaaS upsells, while overall enterprise appetite stops growing. Spend is concentrating, not expanding.

This also explains why investors balked at OpenAI's price and not at AI itself. Money is still pouring into what clearly works. Baseten, Cognition, Cohere and Physical Intelligence all raised or are raising at numbers that would have sounded absurd a year ago. Google Cloud grew 103% to $30.7 billion in one quarter. Demand for compute is real. What the market won't do anymore is pay a trillion-dollar multiple on the theory that consumer chatbot usage turns into revenue on its own.

## Enter the ads

So OpenAI is doing what consumer internet companies have always done when subscriptions stop growing: it's selling your attention. ChatGPT ads launched in February. More than 100,000 advertisers have signed up, and the product recently expanded to 26 more countries. Google pushed ads in AI Mode to 10 more countries last week and says users "find them highly relevant." Advertisers, Wired reports, are "far from satisfied" but expect the experience to improve.

Below the giants, a new ad-tech layer is forming. Koah Labs serves ads across more than 200 AI apps, including DeepAI and Liner, for brands like Hims and UrbanStems. It has roughly a tenth of OpenAI's advertiser count. CEO Nic Baird says ads partly written by AI get double the clicks of fully human-written ones, probably because machine-written copy blends in next to machine-written answers.

Think about what that means for a second. The ads that perform best are the ones you can't tell apart from the answer. That is exactly the incentive search advertising followed for 20 years, and now it runs inside a product people ask whether they should take testosterone therapy. Regulated advertisers like drugmakers have to keep humans in control of their copy, and Baird says their results are weaker. So the market already pays more for less oversight.

## Anthropic's ad-free pledge is a balance-sheet decision

Anthropic made a Super Bowl promise to keep Claude ad-free, and its IPO filing doesn't mention ads at all. Wired's comment is sharp and fair: holding the high ground is easy with about $112 billion of investor cash.

I'd go further. Anthropic can turn down ads because its revenue leans heavily toward enterprise and API, especially coding, where customers pay per token and don't need to be monetized through their eyeballs. OpenAI's base is overwhelmingly free consumers. Ads aren't a moral failing for OpenAI. They follow from the user base it chose to build.

But Anthropic is also inching toward the consumer finance layer. Its new Personal Finance feature connects to your bank through Plaid and gets read-only access to up to two years of transactions, a direct answer to ChatGPT's Finances feature from May. Anthropic's selling point is privacy: by default, conversations in the Personal Finance hub aren't used for training. That's a real difference. It's also the start of the most valuable intent data in consumer tech. A company that can see your spending and your goals doesn't need banner ads. It needs referral fees. The first monetization pressure Anthropic gives in to won't look like an ad. It will look like a "recommended high-yield savings account."

## The bear case, taken seriously

Rory O'Driscoll's view on 20VC is the strongest pushback, and it deserves a hearing. His point: two securities are about to hit the market. One belongs to a larger, faster-growing, profitable company that will soon trade publicly at $1.5 to $2 trillion. The other belongs to a smaller, slower-growing, money-losing, illiquid company. Of course the second prices below the first. Under this reading, the OpenAI markdown is plain relative pricing, not a crack in AI. If demand holds, every GPU gets used and even CoreWeave's 15%-yielding debt turns out fine.

I mostly agree with the mechanics. Where I disagree is on what it implies. If Anthropic becomes the public comparable, every private AI valuation gets marked against quarterly disclosures. That is the real regime change. Private-market AI pricing has run on narrative and scarcity. A public Anthropic swaps both for a 10-Q. The next time Anthropic's growth slows by half, it won't show up in an obscure S-1 amendment. It will show up in an earnings call and move the whole sector in an afternoon.

The other risk signs are real too. Broadcom guaranteeing Anthropic's debt and CoreWeave paying 15% are what an industry looks like when it has financed infrastructure ahead of proven unit economics. Gartner expects data center systems to add $487 billion in 2027 alone, about half of all IT spending growth. That bill comes due whether or not chatbot ads ever get good.

## History rhymes with Facebook, not Pets.com

The right comparison isn't 2000. It's Facebook in 2012. It went public at $38 with a huge user base and an unproven mobile business, and within months the stock had roughly halved. The users weren't the problem. The question was whether attention on a new screen could be monetized. Facebook answered with News Feed ads, and the stock didn't look back.

AI labs are at that moment now. Usage is proven. Monetization per unit of usage isn't. Even Microsoft is tightening up: Microsoft Research's AI Frontiers group, about 50 researchers behind work like AutoGen and the Fara computer-use agent, was folded into Microsoft AI in September and has now effectively been dissolved. When a company with Microsoft's balance sheet stops funding exploratory research to focus on products, the message is clear: the open-ended spending era is over.

## What to Watch

1. **OpenAI's second tranche prices at or below $1.25 trillion.** I don't expect it to fill the full $20 billion before Anthropic's IPO sets a public price.
2. **Anthropic's IPO lands in the $1.5–2 trillion range, but the first-day pop is modest.** "Oversubscribed" headlines will overstate real demand, and the stock will trade on quarterly growth, not the safety story.
3. **Anthropic adds commerce before ads.** Within 12 months of listing, expect affiliate or referral revenue tied to Personal Finance or shopping-style agent tasks. The Super Bowl pledge survives on paper while the definition of "ad" gets narrower.
4. **Per-token list prices keep falling.** Labs will defend revenue by bundling agent work into seats and outcome-based pricing rather than metered tokens.
5. **The chatbot ad-network layer consolidates.** One of Koah's rivals gets acquired by a major ad-tech player or a cloud provider within a year, because whoever owns ad placement inside third-party AI apps owns the next AdSense.
