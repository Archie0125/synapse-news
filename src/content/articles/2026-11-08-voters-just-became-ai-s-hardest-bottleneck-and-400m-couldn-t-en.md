For three years, the AI industry has told us that the limit on its ambitions was physical: not enough Nvidia GPUs, not enough transformers, not enough gigawatts. That story ended this week. The bottleneck that bit hardest on November 3 wasn't silicon or electrons. It was a few million people in school gyms, voting no on the warehouse down the road.

The numbers are lopsided. In what WIRED called "the data center referendum," voters sided with restrictions in nearly every local ballot measure on data centers. WIRED's count is 39 of 40, and other tallies put it at 40 of 41. You can argue about the denominator, but not the direction. Results like that are rare even on puppies and lower taxes. Meanwhile, Elon Musk's America PAC put more than $400 million into the cycle (TechCrunch's Sean O'Kane puts Musk's total giving at close to half a billion) and, in O'Kane's words, got "basically nothing" for it.

My view: local political consent is now a tighter limit on AI infrastructure than chips or power. Unlike chips, a fab can't make more of it.

## How a zoning fight became a national issue

Data center opposition isn't new. Northern Virginia's Loudoun and Prince William counties have argued about it for years. In 2024, Taiwan's Ministry of Economic Affairs effectively stopped approving new data centers above 5MW in the north because the grid couldn't take more load. The research group Data Center Watch counted about $64 billion of US projects blocked or delayed by local opposition between mid-2024 and early 2025.

This year was different because the issue went national. Before, every fight was a separate zoning hearing in a separate county. This cycle, candidates figured out that "data center" works as shorthand for the two themes that decided the election: costs and corruption.

Will Lawrence, a Sunrise Movement co-founder who just beat Republican incumbent Tom Barrett in Michigan's 7th District, put it this way to WIRED: data centers are "taking tax breaks, they're sucking up water, they're proposing to use double or triple the total capacity of the grid in Michigan, they're threatening people's home values." America PAC spent $340,000 against him, and he won anyway.

The pocketbook argument is backed by real numbers. PJM, the grid operator serving about 65 million people from Illinois to New Jersey, saw capacity prices jump from $28.92/MW-day to $269.92/MW-day in its 2025/26 auction, roughly 9x. Data center load forecasts were a big reason. That cost doesn't stay in an earnings call. It shows up on residential power bills, and people vote on their power bills.

## Texas is the result that should worry the industry

If you want a single result to remember, it's Texas. Democrat Gina Hinojosa beat Greg Abbott for governor and has said openly that restricting data centers is something she plans to act on. Texas sold itself to the AI industry with cheap land, the ERCOT grid, and light regulation. Oracle and OpenAI's Stargate campus in Abilene, Musk's companies, and a long line of crypto-turned-AI hosting firms all bet on Texas staying the easy option.

That assumption just broke. No US state can match Texas's mix of land, gas, and space for transmission. If Texas adds friction, there isn't an obvious next place to go.

## The part nobody is talking about: Congress may override the vote

While the coverage focuses on Musk losing, a 417-page bill is moving through the lame-duck Senate. The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA, S.5653) is set for a vote on November 9. Its four sponsors, two Republicans and two Democrats who call themselves the "four corners," want to narrow which projects need federal environmental review, set deadlines on approvals, and limit court challenges.

The bill has real support. The American Clean Power Association says it could unlock 350 GW of clean energy. The White House's Council of Economic Advisers projects GDP gains. Xan Fishman of the Bipartisan Policy Center called it "a really good bipartisan compromise."

Now look at it from the voters' side. NRDC's Christy Goldfuss told The Verge that under the bill, "data centers would not need to answer to the concerns of local communities." Weakening litigation rights and NEPA review removes the main tools local opposition has used.

That sets up a direct conflict. Voters said "slow down, give us a say" by 39-to-1. A lame-duck Congress, whose outgoing members don't face those voters again, could pass a law that cuts down the very channels those voters used. Even if each provision is defensible as energy policy, passing it now would look like confirmation of Lawrence's argument that the system is "rigged." Every data center pitch in 2027 would start from there.

The industry's real choice isn't between building and not building. It's between winning consent and going around it, and going around it is how you end up with more referendums.

## The bear case: this matters less than it looks

Here's the opposing argument, and it deserves a fair hearing.

First, ballot measures are local. A moratorium in one township doesn't stop a hyperscaler with 50 candidate sites. Capital moves, and Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta are spending a combined $350B+ a year on capex. They'll find counties that say yes.

Second, TechCrunch's Kirsten Korosec is right that data centers probably weren't the main cause of the blue wave. Cost of living, the Iran war, and Trump fatigue mattered more. Calling this a "data center election" may be the tech press overrating its own beat.

Third, Democrats aren't against AI. Plenty of Democratic governors want the jobs and tax revenue. Expect conditions, not bans.

All of that is true, and it still misses the point. Even if 49 of 50 sites stay open, the cost of getting to "yes" has risen everywhere. Delays are expensive in AI: a campus that comes online 18 months late runs last-generation chips on an old business plan. And when counties know they hold leverage, they ask for more, including community benefit agreements, water caps, power-cost guarantees, and tax clawbacks. Consent is turning into a line item in the budget, and it's growing faster than GPU prices are falling.

## Companies are already changing course

Korosec noted that companies "like OpenAI all of a sudden go, 'Actually, we can change our policies.'" Pay attention to that. Paying your own grid-upgrade costs, giving up tax abatements, and recycling water in closed-loop systems used to be optional ESG commitments. They're becoming the price of getting approved. Companies that make these concessions early will build. Those that keep trying to win with lawyers and PAC money will keep running into ballot measures.

Musk's spending is the clearest test of the PAC approach, and it failed. Half a billion dollars bought no protection for xAI's Colossus expansions or for his Texas-based companies. If money can't win at the ballot box, the remaining strategy is to show up at the zoning hearing with something residents actually want.

## What to Watch

1. **BAAJA's fate on November 9 and after.** My prediction: it passes the Senate in some form, but the incoming Democratic majority forces data-center-specific carve-outs (local notice requirements or limits on fast-tracking data-center-dedicated generation) before final passage. If it passes unchanged in the lame duck, expect at least five more state-level moratorium bills by mid-2027.
2. **Texas's first move.** Watch whether Hinojosa targets the large-load interconnection rules at ERCOT or the tax abatement program. My bet is interconnection: it's faster, and it hits hardest.
3. **"Bring your own power" becomes standard.** Expect more behind-the-meter gas and nuclear PPAs, with hyperscalers telling regulators "we won't touch your grid." This addresses the cost argument but not the water or land arguments.
4. **Capacity moves offshore.** The UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Malaysia will market themselves as places where neighbors don't get a vote. Expect at least one major US AI lab to announce a new overseas campus of 1GW or more within six months and cite "permitting certainty."
5. **Community benefit agreements become a reported metric.** Within a year, at least one hyperscaler will report community-payment commitments per MW on its earnings calls. When consent shows up on the income statement, it's officially a cost of doing business.
