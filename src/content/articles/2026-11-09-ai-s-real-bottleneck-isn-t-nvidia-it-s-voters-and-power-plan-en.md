For three years, the AI industry measured its ambitions in GPUs. Count the H100s, count the Blackwells, and you knew who was winning. That measure stopped working this month.

Anthropic, a company that sells itself on careful, safety-first AI, has committed $3.4 billion to gas turbines. That makes it a power producer as well as a model lab. A week later, American voters sent Will Lawrence to Congress from Michigan's 7th District. He ran against data centers from the first day of his campaign and beat an incumbent Republican in one of the most expensive House races in the country. Elon Musk's America PAC spent $340,000 trying to stop him and lost.

Taken together, those two events tell you the limit on AI growth has moved. The scarce inputs are now electrons and local permission. Labs can still buy chips. They can't easily buy a town's consent.

## The labs are becoming utilities, and that's a warning sign

When a software company starts buying power plants, it is admitting the grid can't deliver what it needs. Anthropic isn't alone. OpenAI's first Stargate site in Abilene, Texas, has about 820MW of critical IT capacity and is adding on-site gas turbines. xAI ran gas turbines at its Memphis Colossus site and drew sustained local opposition over air quality. SpaceX has started making its own turbine blades because outside suppliers have pushed delivery dates as far out as 2030. Building in-house reportedly cuts generator delays by about 18 months.

So one of the world's leading rocket companies now makes turbine parts to keep AI data centers running. That isn't vertical integration as a strategy. It's a sign the supply chain is badly overloaded.

The second-order effect is the important one. Building your own generation solves the interconnection queue, which in parts of the US means waiting years to connect to the grid. It does nothing about the politics. Behind-the-meter gas makes the political problem worse, because the data center now arrives with its own smokestack. Residents who were already upset about tax breaks and water use now get local emissions too. The labs are swapping a bottleneck they can engineer around for one they can't.

## The midterms were a referendum on who pays

Some post-election commentary says data centers were a minor issue and the media overstated them. That reading is wrong.

Lawrence explained it most clearly: the election came down to "costs and corruption," and data centers touch both. In Michigan, proposed projects would require two or three times the state grid's current total capacity. Voters already understand who covers the cost of that new capacity. In PJM, the largest US grid operator, capacity auction prices rose from $28.92/MW-day for 2024/25 to $269.92 for 2025/26, about a ninefold jump, and then hit the price cap the next year. Data center demand was the main driver, and households saw it on their electric bills.

The ballot results back this up. Local measures to restrict data centers or impose moratoriums passed almost everywhere they appeared. Texas, which data center developers had treated as the most reliable place to build in America, elected Gina Hinojosa over Greg Abbott, and restricting data centers was one of her campaign promises. The state where Stargate and Musk's businesses are based now has a governor who campaigned against the industry's growth plans.

Musk gave around half a billion dollars to Republicans and got almost nothing back. The lesson for the rest of the industry is that national political spending doesn't buy local approval. Zoning boards make that call.

## Here's what nobody is talking about: China doesn't have this problem

Coverage of the US-China AI race focuses on chips: export controls, Huawei Ascend yields, smuggled GPUs. Very little of it covers how each country builds data centers, and that may matter more.

SemiAnalysis visited Envision's Galaxy campus in Ulanqab, Inner Mongolia, in September. The first 120MW went from start to operation in under a year, at roughly one-fifth the cost of comparable US projects. The full campus is planned for 800MW of IT capacity by the end of 2027, which is about the size of Abilene.

Envision isn't waiting for the grid or ordering gas turbines that won't arrive for years. It built 420MW of wind farms for an expected 240MW load from the first two phases, oversizing because Ulanqab's wind capacity factor is only 35–40%. It added four-hour battery storage to cover calm periods. Inside, solid-state transformers convert 10kV AC directly to 800V DC at the rack, which removes several conversion steps that US facilities still use.

This is about more than cost. Envision has installed over 100GW of wind turbines worldwide, so it controls its own generation supply chain. American labs are lining up behind GE Vernova and Siemens Energy for gas turbines. And no biker gang in Ulanqab is going to block the project at a planning hearing.

That last point matters most. The US problem isn't only engineering. In a democracy, people can say no, and Americans just showed they will. China's AI build-out is limited by chips. America's is increasingly limited by consent. Those are different races, and the US is treating them as one.

## The backlash has gone global, and the coalitions are strange

In Thurrock, 15 miles east of London, a petition against Google's planned data center has nearly 55,000 signatures. The opposition includes Buglife, which is worried about a rare bumblebee; Foxglove, a tech-accountability nonprofit focused on pollution; and the Thurrock Hammers motorcycle club, which wants its racing site back. In Amsterdam, protesters climbed a crane at a Microsoft data center site. The UK government is promoting "AI Growth Zones," and the people who live in them are organizing against them.

When insect conservationists, privacy activists and a motorcycle club are on the same side, the opposition isn't ideological. It's local, and local opposition is the hardest kind to beat. Arguing about national competitiveness and the race with China doesn't persuade someone who's worried about their property value and their electric bill.

## The climate argument hasn't held up

The industry's best defense used to be that AI would pay back its energy use by helping solve climate change. In 2024, Sam Altman promised AI would end up "fixing the climate." In 2023, Google's sustainability head co-wrote that AI could cut global emissions 5–10% by 2030.

As COP31 meets in Antalya, MIT Technology Review checked those claims against what companies are actually doing. A peer-reviewed LSE Grantham/Systemiq study estimated AI could cut 3.2–5.4 billion tons of CO₂ a year by 2035, concentrated in power, meat and dairy, and vehicles. Very few of the large companies in those sectors have announced the specific measures behind that estimate, and even fewer report results. The emissions savings are still projections. The gas turbines are real and on order.

That matters politically. Without a credible climate argument, the industry's main pitch is national competitiveness, and that pitch doesn't work on people at local zoning meetings.

## The bear case

Here's the strongest argument against my position. The backlash may never stop AI from scaling. It may only push it elsewhere: to Wyoming and North Dakota, the UAE, Malaysia, Nordic countries with surplus hydro, or Inner Mongolia. Capital will move to wherever approval is easiest. The midterm results could make the US less important for data centers without slowing the industry overall. Efficiency gains, like DeepSeek's 2025 shock, could also cut power demand enough to ease the pressure.

I partly agree. Geographic spread will happen. But latency, data sovereignty rules and export controls limit how much compute can leave the US, and frontier training needs gigawatt-scale campuses that only a few places can support. Some of the industry will relocate. The rest will have to negotiate with voters.

## What to Watch

1. **Texas will impose a large-load fee within its first 2027 legislative session.** Expect Hinojosa to push for data centers to pay the full cost of grid upgrades and to face mandatory curtailment during peak demand. Developers will claim this will drive them out of the state. Most will stay, because the alternatives are worse.

2. **"Ratepayer guarantees" will become standard in deal announcements.** OpenAI is already changing its policies. Within six months, expect every major lab to publish commitments that its data centers won't raise residential power rates. Some will be real. Others will be clever accounting.

3. **At least one more frontier lab will announce a power-generation deal worth over $1B by mid-2027.** After Anthropic's $3.4B, turbine supply contracts will matter as much competitively as Nvidia allocations.

4. **Galaxy's cost per MW will become a talking point in Washington.** When the next round of export-control debates begins, expect the one-fifth-cost figure to be used as an argument for permitting reform. It will run straight into the new Congress members who were elected to oppose that kind of reform.

5. **Gas-fired AI campuses will become the next target.** Opposition so far has focused on water and tax breaks. The next wave will focus on air permits for on-site turbines, and that fight will move from city councils to state environmental agencies.

AI's biggest constraint in 2027 won't be in a fab in Hsinchu. It will be in a zoning meeting in Michigan.
