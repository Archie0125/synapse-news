The most damaging sentence in the Nvidia scandal wasn't written by Jensen Huang. It came from *The New York Times*, which reported that US intelligence officials briefed President Trump about Alice Huang roughly a year ago. According to the same reporting, nobody gave the CEO of the world's most strategic chip company a "defensive briefing," the routine warning counterintelligence agencies give to people they believe are being targeted.

Hold onto that, because the rest of the story is getting told as a morality play. In that version, a beloved founder falls, a board scrambles, and Congress demands answers. That version is easier to tell. It also misses most of what's going on.

## What actually happened, and what didn't

Start with what's established. On Sunday, November 8, Huang sent Nvidia staff a letter admitting he had been unfaithful to his wife, Lori, to whom he's been married for 41 years. On the same day, Nvidia's board said a committee of independent directors, working with outside counsel, would investigate claims that Huang "has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort." The board also said it had "no reason to believe" that national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised, and that Huang stays in the job.

Here's what is alleged, and only alleged. The *Times*, the *Financial Times* and *The Wall Street Journal*, all citing unnamed US officials, report that Alice Huang (also known as Huang Le, no relation to Jensen) was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security to gather intelligence on him. She was an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore data center company the Commerce Department has been investigating since 2024 over claims that it moved about $2 billion of restricted Nvidia chips into China through a subsidiary called Speedmatrix. The FT reports that US officials believe the two were romantically involved. No one has been charged, and China's embassy calls the reports "groundless smears."

Now read Huang's letter closely. It admits infidelity. It never names Alice Huang. It sends the national security questions to the board's investigation and ends with "I am proud and grateful to be an American." This is a carefully lawyered document. It concedes the personal failing, which can't be denied and isn't a crime, and walls it off from the national security question, which could end his career. Whether the two can stay walled off is the question everything else depends on.

## The angle nobody is talking about: the government sat on it

Look at the timeline as US officials have described it. Intelligence agencies identified Alice Huang as an asset. They briefed the president about a year ago. At that point, according to the *Times*, officials didn't think Jensen Huang knew. Then they apparently didn't tell him.

There are only two ways to explain that, and both look bad.

**Explanation one: it was a deliberate counterintelligence choice.** Agencies sometimes leave a known asset in place to watch who she talks to, what she asks for, and where the information goes. If that's what happened, Washington knowingly let a suspected MSS asset keep close access to the man who decides which chips Nvidia builds, during the exact period when the H20 ban, the 15% revenue-share deal and the debate over H200 sales to China were all being settled. In other words, the government used Nvidia's CEO as unwitting bait while those decisions were being made.

**Explanation two: it was a failure.** The information sat inside the system and nobody was assigned to act on it. If so, the export-control regime, which already has a Commerce Department enforcement office widely described as understaffed for the volume of chips flowing through Southeast Asia, has an intelligence-sharing gap as well as a smuggling gap.

Congress is calling in Huang. The more useful hearing would put the FBI and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence in the same room and ask why the defensive briefing never happened. That question is mostly missing from Moolenaar, Mast and Crawford's letter.

## Why Megaspeed is the real story

Strip out the romance and you're left with a well-documented structural problem: **Singapore**. In fiscal 2025, Nvidia billed roughly $23.7 billion of revenue to customers in Singapore, about 18% of its total. Nvidia has said less than 2% of that hardware actually shipped to Singapore. Most of it is billing addresses for global companies. That's a legitimate practice, but it creates exactly the paper trail a smuggler would hide behind.

Megaspeed reportedly kept buying billions of dollars of Nvidia hardware even after Commerce opened its investigation. Bain Capital's data center unit removed Megaspeed as a tenant. Nvidia has publicly said Megaspeed had no China ties. If the board's investigation finds that Nvidia's sales and compliance teams kept serving a customer under federal investigation while the CEO was personally close to one of its executives, that becomes a governance finding, and it doesn't depend on whether any secrets leaked.

This is the second-order effect that matters most. For two years, Huang has been the most effective lobbyist in Washington for selling to China. His argument was simple: controls push Chinese buyers toward Huawei's Ascend chips, and Nvidia's China data center share has already collapsed from around 95% toward zero. Whatever you think of that argument, its credibility depended on the messenger being seen as a patriotic American founder with no conflicts. That messenger is now compromised in the political sense, even if he's never compromised in the intelligence sense.

## The comparisons don't fit neatly

Corporate history offers templates, and none of them fit cleanly. Intel's Brian Krzanich resigned in 2018 over a consensual relationship with an employee. McDonald's pushed out Steve Easterbrook in 2019. BP's Bernard Looney left in 2023 for failing to disclose relationships. In each case, the board enforced a conduct policy against a professional manager.

Huang isn't a professional manager. He co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and has run it for 33 years. The company is worth more than $4 trillion and posted roughly $216 billion in revenue in its last fiscal year. Its 10-K lists dependence on him as a risk factor. Yale's Jeffrey Sonnenfeld puts his odds of survival at "about 50-50" and argues that "any employee in Nvidia other than him would be fired over this." That's true, and it's also the point. The board isn't asking whether Huang broke a rule. It's asking what Nvidia is without him.

The closer comparison is political: the 2020 reporting that a suspected Chinese intelligence operative, Christine Fang, had cultivated relationships with US politicians including Rep. Eric Swalwell. Swalwell was never charged and kept his seat. A House committee seat, though, isn't a $4 trillion company sitting at the center of a trade war.

## The bear case, taken seriously

Here's the strongest argument that I'm wrong to predict he survives. Unnamed senior Trump officials have already told the *Times* they doubt Huang could have stayed unaware of Alice Huang's role for that long. If the investigation finds that he was told, or should have known, and kept up the relationship anyway, Sonnenfeld's "idiosyncrasy credits" run out overnight. Institutional shareholders at BlackRock and Vanguard, who answer to their own regulators, will want a transition.

There's an equally serious bear case in the other direction: the market is overreacting. CUDA's lock-in, the Blackwell-to-Rubin roadmap, and TSMC capacity contracts signed years ahead don't depend on one man's personal life. Nvidia's engineering bench is deep. Jay Puri on the sales side and Colette Kress in finance have run very large parts of the business for years. Even a forced succession wouldn't stop the product cadence.

I believe the second argument more than the first, but only on the product side. On policy, Nvidia without a trusted Huang in Washington is a weaker company, and that cost is real.

## What to Watch

- **Huang stays, but loses some power.** I expect the investigation to end within about 90 days with no finding of a confirmed leak, and Huang still CEO. I also expect it to recommend something Nvidia has avoided for 33 years: a formal successor or a COO with real authority, plus a lead independent director with teeth.
- **H200 China licenses stall.** Any pending expansion of Nvidia sales to China freezes through the end of the investigation. Hawks will use the moment to revive the Chip Security Act's location-verification requirements, and they'll have the votes.
- **Singapore gets squeezed.** Expect tighter end-user verification for Southeast Asian data center buyers. Malaysia and Thailand will draw the same scrutiny, and Nvidia's reported Singapore billing figure will become a quarterly talking point on earnings calls.
- **The FBI question surfaces.** Within six months, at least one Democratic member of the Intelligence Committee will formally ask why no defensive briefing was given. That's the hearing that will change policy.
- **Every AI CEO gets a briefing.** OpenAI, AMD, TSMC Arizona and the hyperscalers will quietly expand executive counterintelligence programs. Being "targeted by a foreign intelligence effort" is now treated as a risk that comes with the job.
