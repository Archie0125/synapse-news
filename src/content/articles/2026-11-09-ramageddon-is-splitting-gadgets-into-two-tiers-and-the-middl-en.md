Phone makers are now melting memory chips off dead phones and soldering them into new ones. A recycling trick from Shenzhen repair stalls has become a supply-chain strategy. That tells you more about where consumer tech is in November 2026 than any keynote this year.

The industry has started calling it RAMageddon, and the name fits. For three years, HBM for Nvidia accelerators and server DRAM for AI clusters have pulled wafer capacity away from the LPDDR and DDR chips that go into everything else. The bill has now reached your living room, your bag and your pocket, and it's arriving in three forms: price hikes, spec cuts and cancellations.

## The receipts

Here is what has happened to ordinary devices in the last few months:

- **Google Pixel 10a:** launched at $499 and was raised to $599 in early October, a 20% jump on a phone whose whole appeal is value. Woot's current $449 deal looks like a steal only because the reference price moved.
- **Walmart's Onn Google TV streamers:** a second round of increases this year, $10 on every model. The cheapest stick, $15 before 2026, now costs about $40. The Onn FHD stick went from $29.98 to $39.98, and the 4K Pro from $59.98 to $69.98. These boxes carry 1.5GB to 2GB of RAM. When devices with that little memory get repriced, every product with a DRAM chip is exposed.
- **Amazon Kindle:** the October refresh came with $40 to $50 increases across the lineup. The ad-free 6-inch basic model is now $169.99, about 30% more than the $129.99 its predecessor cost in 2024. It's still the cheapest way into Kindle.
- **Oppo Find X10 Ultra:** Weibo leaker Digital Chat Station reports that it's been cancelled. It was due to carry a 200MP 1/1.12-inch main sensor with LOFIC, a 200MP periscope and a 50MP 10x telephoto, which would have made it one of the most ambitious camera phones of the cycle. Oppo's current top model, the Find X10 Pro Max, already moved from the X9 Ultra's Snapdragon and 16GB of RAM to a MediaTek chip with 12GB in most variants.

Put these side by side and you can see the pattern. The shortage isn't hitting every product equally. It's cutting the two ends of the market and squeezing the middle.

## Why now, and why it's different from 2018

Memory has always been cyclical. In the 2017–2018 supercycle, DRAM prices roughly doubled and Samsung posted record profits. In 2022–2023 the market crashed so badly that Samsung's memory division lost billions and SK hynix and Micron cut production. Consumers have seen expensive RAM before.

This time the buyer on the other side is different. In earlier cycles, demand from phones, PCs and servers rose and fell more or less together. Now there's one class of buyer with almost no price sensitivity: hyperscalers and AI labs. In October 2025, OpenAI signed letters of intent with Samsung and SK hynix for up to 900,000 DRAM wafers a month for Stargate, a number analysts compared to roughly 40% of global DRAM output. Whatever share of that turns into firm orders, it told memory makers where the margins are.

HBM makes the squeeze worse. Each bit of HBM uses about three times the wafer area of standard DRAM, so every line converted to HBM removes more consumer supply than the headline capacity suggests. Micron went as far as winding down its Crucial consumer brand to put supply where the money is. When the third-largest DRAM maker in the world decides retail buyers aren't worth serving, a $40 streaming stick won't win an allocation fight against a GPU cluster.

## What nobody is talking about: the AI boom is starving on-device AI

This is the irony that should worry Google, Samsung and Apple more than the price tags.

For two years, the phone industry's main pitch has been on-device AI: Gemini Nano, Galaxy AI, Apple Intelligence. All of it depends on RAM. Running a capable local model means keeping billions of parameters resident in memory, which is why 12GB became the practical minimum for flagship AI features and why 16GB looked like the next standard.

Now look at Oppo stepping back from 16GB to 12GB. Look at the budget market, where TrendForce says reballed 4GB LPDDR4X chips are going into 4G phones priced under CNY 1,299 (about $200). The cloud AI boom is using up the memory the edge AI story needs. If RAM per device stalls or falls for two years, on-device AI stays a flagship-only feature, and more inference moves back to the cloud, which is exactly the demand that created the shortage in the first place. Hyperscalers win both ways.

## Reballing: clever, green, and a warranty problem

TrendForce puts reballed memory at 3–4% of smartphones today and expects 7–9% in 2027. It also expects DRAM contract prices to keep rising in Q1 2027, and says reballing could spread beyond entry-level phones.

On the positive side, it's the most practical e-waste reduction the phone industry has managed in years, and it keeps a $150 phone at $150. On the negative side, these chips have already been through heat cycles. Reballing adds more thermal stress, and LPDDR4X is a generation behind. Few buyers will know which memory is in their phone, and almost no spec sheet will say. Expect more early failures in budget phones sold in markets with weak warranty enforcement. The cost savings show up on the maker's books, while the risk ends up with buyers in India, Southeast Asia, Africa and Latin America.

Tom's Hardware also reports that hobbyists are now turning $120 laptop SO-DIMM kits into desktop DDR5 sticks worth around $1,000. When enthusiasts are reworking DRAM at home to save money, the consumer memory market isn't working properly.

## The middle gets reset, and it won't fully come back

The Pixel 10a is the clearest example of the long-term effect. At $599, Google's "affordable" phone costs what a Pixel 7 did at launch in 2022. Samsung's Galaxy A5x line and Apple's iPhone e series will be measured against that new anchor. The likely result is that $599 becomes the normal price for the mid-range tier, with $449 promotions sold as bargains.

Prices in consumer electronics are sticky. When memory costs fall, brands rarely cut list prices. They add spec bumps at the same price and keep the margin. The Onn stick that cost $15 won't go back to $15. At most it returns to $30 with a new remote.

## The bear case

The opposing argument deserves a fair hearing: memory always overshoots. SK hynix's M15X fab, Micron's Idaho expansion and Samsung's Pyeongtaek P4 lines are all bringing capacity online through 2027–2028. If AI capex growth slows, even for two quarters, after a disappointing earnings season, contract DRAM prices could fall as fast as they did in 2022. In that case, phone makers that locked in long-term contracts at peak prices would be the ones in trouble, and Oppo's cancellation would look like panic.

That's possible, but the timing works against consumers. New fabs take 18–24 months to ramp, and their first output will go to HBM because that's what justified building them. Consumer relief comes last in any recovery. Expect it in 2028, not 2027.

## What to Watch

1. **Ultras disappearing:** at least two more Android brands will cancel or delay a 2027 "Ultra" or camera flagship before Mobile World Congress in March. Watch Vivo's X-series Ultra and Xiaomi's international Ultra launch.
2. **Pixel 11a at $599 or higher at launch:** Google won't go back to $499. The October hike was a test, and the market accepted it.
3. **Stalled RAM specs:** most 2027 mid-range Android phones will ship with 8GB, the same as 2024. Apple will hold the iPhone 18 base model at 8GB or 12GB, and AI features will be split by tier.
4. **Reballing disclosure:** the first lawsuit or regulatory action over undisclosed recycled memory will come from India or the EU by late 2027.
5. **Amazon holds the new Kindle prices** through Prime Day 2027, and discounts won't go below 2024 list prices. Memory-driven price hikes have become the default for the industry.

The AI boom was supposed to make your devices smarter. For the next 18 months, its main effect on them will be higher prices.
