What if a robot could be squishy? Soft, flexible robots could be useful for a wide variety of applications, including artificial muscles and [wearable devices](https://www.wired.com/story/why-the-hottest-new-wearables-want-to-be-ignored/)—or really any application where a robot must be [especially delicate](https://www.wired.com/story/an-ai-agent-did-brain-surgery-on-live-mouse/).

This is what has fueled development of “soft actuators” that can flex like a bicep and have a fine-tuned sense of touch as in the [human hand](https://www.wired.com/story/the-1x-neo-robot-has-freaky-fast-fingers/). Like your stereotypical robot, soft actuators convert energy—such as electricity, heat, or light—into mechanical motion, but they look nothing like the metal and wire constructs that the word “robot” most often evokes.

One variety of these soft robots, known as bioactuators, utilizes the functions of living cells to generate motion. These biological machines have attracted particular attention in recent years, and now a research team at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands has [developed](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-78653-z) a soft robotic arm [colonized by algae](https://www.wired.com/story/reflecting-pool-full-of-algae-climate-change/) on its surface, and when the microbes photosynthesize, the arm moves.

“We didn’t start with a specific application. Our goal has always been to bring the research of living materials to new levels,” Marie-Eve Aubin-Tam, who led the research, explained in [a press release](https://www.tudelft.nl/en/2026/tnw/soft-robot-arm-powered-by-algae). “We wanted to explore what becomes possible when living cells are integrated into functional materials.”

The tiny robot arms—about three-quarters of an inch long and about as thick as a credit card—are constructed of two layers of hydrogel that the [algal cells live on](https://www.wired.com/story/tiny-hairs-that-help-corals-breathe-may-malfunction-in-warming-oceans/). One of these layers responds to changes in pH, which are mediated by the metabolism of the algae. When light strikes the algae, they begin to photosynthesize. By absorbing carbon dioxide and fixing nitrogen in this process, the algae make the local pH more basic, making the responsive hydrogel swell. Conversely, when it becomes dark, the algae release carbon dioxide through respiration, returning the pH to the acidic side and the hydrogel shrinks.

The difference in the volumes of the two [hydrogel layers](https://www.wired.com/story/researchers-create-3d-printed-artificial-skin-that-allows-blood-circulation/) controls the bending. According to the researchers, the pH-responsive hydrogel layer changed significantly with the algae’s activity. In acidic conditions, such as between pH 3 and 7, the layer was approximately 37 percent of its original weight, but it had quadruple its original weight at pH 11.

Other researchers have constructed soft robots that respond to pH, but typically the chemistry of all the surrounding liquid has to be altered. This is not the case for the algal bots. The photosynthetic activity has an effect that’s much more local, meaning it would be better suited to applications where minimal environmental impact is essential.

These aren’t, however, the speediest of robots. The chemical shift begins quickly, about 20 seconds after light is applied and about 10 seconds after it becomes dark, but it takes several hours for the hydrogel to bend significantly.

After their first proof of concept, the research team constructed several more algal devices. One was a four-fingered gripper that closes when exposed to light. It successfully grasped and lifted a hollow metal sphere, releasing it when the light was turned off. Another hook-shaped gripper could pick up and put down a rubber ring. These items were extremely light, weighing far less than a small paperclip, but the arms were able to grasp them with [great dexterity](https://www.wired.com/story/this-robot-is-going-to-replace-your-interns-flexion/).

These soft robots also require no external energy source other than light and no complex mechanical parts. “Microalgae are plantlike organisms that are largely self-sustaining,” Aubin-Tam said. This “gives the actuator potential for long-term operation with minimal external input. In theory, it wouldn’t require a lot of energy to run a system based on this technology.”

In the long term, the research team hopes this will contribute to the development of more autonomous, environment-responsive soft robots that could be used as automated agricultural equipment or for fluid control in lab-on-a-chip devices or really as part of any underwater robotics system.

Since the algae do not need to be [genetically modified](https://www.wired.com/story/cool-cool-cool-palmer-luckey-made-a-genetically-modified-military-dog-startup/), there are none of the biosafety concerns often associated with microbe-based technologies. If anything, these are exceptionally [environmentally friendly](https://www.wired.com/story/how-to-buy-ethical-and-eco-friendly-electronics/) machines, since these organisms can absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere while deriving energy from sunlight.

## Summary

**TLDR:** Algae-powered robot arms are a striking proof of concept, but by the team's own admission there's no application yet. That's the honest part.

- Researchers at Delft University of Technology built a soft robotic arm with living algae on its surface; when the algae photosynthesize, the arm moves.
- Each arm is about three-quarters of an inch long, roughly as thick as a credit card, and made of two hydrogel layers that the algae live on.
- The work was published in Nature Communications and is part of a wider push for 'bioactuators,' soft robots that use living cells to create motion.
- Lead researcher Marie-Eve Aubin-Tam says the team did not start with an application in mind. This is basic materials research, not a product.
- The long-term pitch is light-driven motion without batteries or wiring, which could suit artificial muscles, wearables or delicate handling. Speed, force and durability are still unanswered.

## 摘要

**重點摘要：** 用藻類驅動的機器手臂是很吸睛的概念驗證，但團隊自己也承認目前還沒有具體應用；這份坦白反而最有價值。

- 荷蘭台夫特理工大學團隊做出一種表面長滿藻類的軟體機器手臂，藻類一行光合作用，手臂就會動。
- 手臂長約 2 公分、厚度和信用卡差不多，由兩層水凝膠構成，藻類就住在上面。
- 研究刊登於《Nature Communications》，屬於近年受到關注的「生物致動器」領域，也就是用活細胞產生動作的軟體機器人。
- 主持研究的 Marie-Eve Aubin-Tam 坦言，團隊一開始就沒有鎖定應用，目標是推進活體材料研究，離產品化還很遠。
- 長期想像是「照光就能動」，不需要電池和線路，可用在人工肌肉、穿戴裝置或需要極度輕柔的操作上。不過速度、力道和壽命都還沒有答案。
