The US government is examining whether [Waymo](https://www.wired.com/tag/waymo/)’s recall of software involved with a series of [school bus passing incidents last fall](https://www.wired.com/story/a-school-district-tried-to-help-train-waymos-to-stop-for-school-buses-it-didnt-work/) actually fixed the safety issue.

The formal investigation by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration will determine whether the company’s supposed software fix succeeded in preventing its robotaxis from improperly passing stopped school buses with red lights on and their stop arms extended—indicators that the buses were picking up and dropping off students. It is illegal to pass these stopped school buses in all 50 states.

According to a filing, Waymo issued a fix for the software issue in mid-November and a voluntary recall a month later. But the government says that the majority of school bus issues occurred after that date.

Earlier this year, a [report](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/Pages/HWY26FH007.aspx) from the National Transportation Safety Board, the US’s independent safety agency, found that another school bus passing incident occurred in mid-January, several weeks after the supposed software fix. The NTSB is also investigating the school bus passing issue.

In a written statement, Waymo spokesperson Dana Gallagher said the company has “made significant improvements to vehicle behavior around school buses” and that it has not been cited for passing school buses since March—months after the mid-December software recall. She wrote that Waymo looks “forward to continuing \[its\] engagement on recall processes for AV software that continues to learn and improve.”

Late last year, [WIRED reported](https://www.wired.com/story/a-school-district-tried-to-help-train-waymos-to-stop-for-school-buses-it-didnt-work/), the Austin, Texas, school district even helped set up a half-day data collection event for Waymo’s vehicles, coordinating with the self-driving vehicle developer to bring school buses and stop-arm signals to an empty parking lot so that the company could collect information to help train its vehicles to avoid the illegal street maneuver. The data collection event occurred after Waymo had issued its software fix and voluntary recall. Still, the incidents continued.

In years past, recall queries like this one have [led to civil penalties](https://www.nhtsa.gov/laws-regulations/civil-penalty-settlements), some of them costing automakers and software developers tens of millions of dollars.

## Summary

**TLDR:** Waymo's school bus recall exposes the core problem with AV safety: a software fix is only a claim until real roads prove it.

- NHTSA opened a formal investigation into whether Waymo's software recall stopped its robotaxis from illegally passing stopped school buses.
- Waymo issued a fix in mid-November and a voluntary recall a month later, but the government says most incidents occurred after that.
- An NTSB report found another school bus passing incident in mid-January, weeks after the supposed fix.
- Waymo says it has improved behavior and has not been cited for passing school buses since March.
- The case could shape how regulators handle recalls for continuously learning AV software.

## 摘要

**重點摘要：** Waymo 的校車召回暴露出自駕車安全的核心問題：軟體修正在真實道路驗證之前，都只是宣稱。

- 美國國家公路交通安全管理局（NHTSA）正式調查 Waymo 的軟體召回，是否真能阻止其自駕計程車違法超越停靠中的校車。
- Waymo 於 11 月中推出修正，一個月後自願召回，但政府指出多數事件發生在召回之後。
- 國家運輸安全委員會（NTSB）報告發現，所謂的修正推出數週後，1 月中又發生一起超越校車事件。
- Waymo 表示已大幅改善車輛行為，自 3 月以來未再因超越校車被開罰。
- 此案可能影響監管機關如何處理持續學習型自駕軟體的召回流程。
