The most important AI number this week isn't a benchmark score. It's 81 percent: the share of Michigan voters who just approved a ballot measure banning political donations from utilities. The measure barely mentions artificial intelligence, yet it may do more to shape the AI buildout than any model release this quarter.

The story the industry tells about itself goes: chips are scarce, models are hungry, so build faster. The story actually playing out on the ground is different. **Electrons and permission are now scarcer than GPUs**, and the companies that understand this are acting like utilities, real estate developers and political operatives all at the same time.

## The labs are becoming power companies

Look at what frontier labs are spending on. Anthropic's $3.4 billion gas-turbine deal is not an AI purchase in any usual sense. It is an energy purchase made by a software company. SpaceX's AI arm, the old xAI, has filed for a $24 billion gas-fired power plant and supercomputing campus inside the city limits of Starbase, Texas. That $24 billion covers only the "initial phases," and it feeds a stated goal of 10 gigawatts of compute in 2027 alone. OpenAI's Project Camellia in Effingham County, Georgia, is planned at 3.2 gigawatts across five phases for $20 to $30 billion.

For scale, 3.2 GW is about the output of three large nuclear reactors. One OpenAI campus in one Georgia county would draw on the order of a mid-sized American city.

None of this is new as an idea. In 2024 Microsoft signed a 20-year deal to restart Three Mile Island Unit 1. Amazon bought a data center campus attached to Talen's Susquehanna nuclear plant for $650 million. Google signed with Kairos Power for small modular reactors. What has changed is the fuel. Nuclear was the clean, slow, PR-friendly option. **Gas is the fast and politically radioactive one.** Labs are picking gas because nuclear can't arrive before 2030, and in this race 2030 means you've already lost.

## Why behind-the-meter power doesn't actually escape the politics

The case for building your own power is simple. If you generate on site, you skip the multi-year grid interconnection queue, and you can tell regulators you aren't raising anyone's electric bill.

That second claim matters a lot right now. Michiganders' bills rose 10 percent in the last 12 months, the fourth-largest increase in the U.S. DTE has proposed two new gas plants costing $6.4 billion to serve expected data center load, and ordinary ratepayers could end up paying for them. At least 26 data center projects were planned across Michigan as of July. Voters noticed. Rate hikes, utility influence and data centers all helped drive this month's election results.

Behind-the-meter generation looks like a way out: we pay for our own power, so your bill isn't our problem. OpenAI is making a version of that promise in Georgia, saying it will cover the full cost of grid upgrades and use a closed-loop water system.

Here's the catch. **Leaving the electricity-rate fight puts you in the air-quality fight.** xAI's Colossus sites near Memphis and the Mississippi line already show how that goes. Residents sued over the turbine fleet's emissions, and a judge just refused to dismiss the case, ruling that the Mississippi gas turbines are not exempt from the Clean Air Act. "Temporary" turbines turned out to be permanent legal exposure.

So the labs are swapping one form of opposition for another. Ratepayer anger is spread thin and moves slowly through utility commissions. Pollution anger is concentrated, has plaintiffs and comes with a federal statute to sue under.

## What nobody is talking about: the backlash has changed targets

The coverage frames this as "locals vs. data centers." That's already out of date. Opposition has moved past fighting individual buildings and is now going after **the process that approves them.**

Read the Effingham County lawsuit closely. Residents Dr. Michelle Finch-Mincey, Lehman Mincey Jr. and Raymond Carver filed a RICO suit, the racketeering statute written for the Mafia. It names all six county commissioners, the county manager, the Industrial Development Authority and its CEO, OpenAI and its operating entity Octans GA LLC. The complaint is mostly not about noise or water. It's about **NDAs**. The plaintiffs allege that confidentiality agreements and misleading public statements kept residents uninformed until the land sales, tax incentives and zoning were already locked in.

Under Georgia's RICO law, which is looser than the federal version, that theory could survive a motion to dismiss even if it fails at trial. Either way, the industry's standard playbook is now evidence. For a decade, hyperscalers have entered towns under codenames like "Project Camellia" through shell LLCs with officials sworn to secrecy. That worked when a data center meant 50 megawatts and a few hundred jobs. It doesn't work at 3.2 gigawatts.

Michigan's Proposition 2 is the same move one level up. It doesn't stop any data center. It cuts off the political money of the utilities that lobbied for data center tax breaks, and it covers state contractors with over $250,000 a year in government contracts plus their executives, lobbyists and immediate families. Consumers Energy and DTE are suing on First and Fourteenth Amendment grounds. David Pomerantz of the Energy & Policy Institute calls Michigan "ground zero" for a national utility accountability wave.

The pattern across both cases: **voters have figured out that the data center is a symptom and the deal-making is the disease.** That's a much harder opponent, because you can't fix it by moving the building one county over.

## Starbase: the logical endgame

If the obstacle is community consent, the cleanest fix is to own the community. That's essentially what Starbase is. Most of the town sits on SpaceX land and most residents are SpaceX employees. A public hearing in a company town is a formality.

Even that isn't a sure bet. Incoming Texas governor Gina Hinojosa campaigned on pausing new data center approvals until the legislature passes stronger protections. Texas already halted new data centers in August pending audits. When Texas, the most permissive major energy market in the country, starts putting conditions on data centers, the "just go somewhere friendlier" strategy runs out of places.

## The money is already pricing it in

The financial side tells the same story. CoreWeave's Q3 results show the market now **prices AI compute per megawatt.** Short-term capacity is selling for more than $40 million per megawatt per year, while four- to five-year contracts on new builds run $20 to $25 million. CoreWeave raised prices about 25 percent in July and another 10 percent later in the quarter. It expects roughly $9 billion in customer prepayments over the next three months, and Nebius and IREN are also funded by big Microsoft prepayments.

So what? When short-term power-backed capacity sells at roughly double the long-term rate, the scarce asset isn't silicon. It's **energized, permitted megawatts available now.** Every lawsuit, ballot measure and gubernatorial pause adds to that premium. Local opposition is a cost, but for whoever already has powered capacity, it's also a moat.

It's a fragile one. CoreWeave reported $3.66 billion in revenue, up 168 percent, along with a $709 million net loss and $846 million in quarterly interest expense. It's funding a $132.5 billion backlog with contract-backed loans because, in its CEO's words, it won't use unsecured debt "at current yields."

## The bear case

The strongest counterargument: this is noise. Lawsuits get dismissed, and RICO claims against local governments rarely win. An 81 percent ballot result shows anger but stops no projects. Ascend Analytics' Brent Nelson argues that many announced data centers are press releases, not projects. If that's true, some of the backlash is aimed at buildings that would never have existed, and the real build rate is well below the headlines. Gas turbines are also a sensible bridge, since U.S. gas is cheap and plentiful.

I accept the last two points and reject the conclusion. Phantom projects make the backlash worse, because every speculative announcement brings NDAs, tax abatements and rezonings that anger residents whether or not anything gets built. The industry is spending political capital on projects it won't finish.

## What to Watch

- **Copycat ballot measures by 2028.** At least two more states with heavy data center pipelines, likely Georgia, Virginia, Arizona or Ohio, will put utility political-spending limits on the ballot. Michigan's 81 percent is a template campaigners won't ignore.
- **NDA disclosure becomes standard.** Whatever happens to the Effingham RICO claims, by the end of 2027 at least one major lab or hyperscaler will publicly drop confidential pre-announcement agreements with local officials. Discovery risk will cost more than the secrecy is worth.
- **Starbase gets local approval in December, then the fight moves to Austin.** The company town will say yes. The real test is whether Hinojosa's administration uses the state's environmental permitting power on the gas plant.
- **The Memphis Clean Air Act case becomes the template for every behind-the-meter turbine fleet.** Expect copycat suits against on-site gas generation within 12 months, which will push labs toward cleaner but slower options like fuel cells, batteries and nuclear PPAs.
- **Short-term compute tops $50M per megawatt per year** in 2027 as permitted capacity falls further behind demand. The winners of this cycle won't be whoever has the most GPUs but whoever has the most megawatts that already have signed approvals.

The AI race was supposed to come down to who builds the smartest model. Increasingly, it comes down to who can win a county commission vote without getting sued for racketeering.
