Ignore the affairs for a minute. The most important fact in the Jensen Huang story is that after 33 years, nobody can name the person who would run Nvidia next week.

That is a stranger problem than the tabloid version. Nvidia is the company that Washington's export-control policy, every hyperscaler's capex plan and most of the AI startup economy depend on. Its board has apparently never shown investors a successor, and it is now reportedly deciding whether to fire its only CEO, live, while Congress drafts a subpoena. The stock has fallen about 20% since the scandal broke, including a 7–8% drop in after-hours trading on Wednesday. That isn't a morality discount. It's what a company built around one person looks like when that person is suddenly in question.

## How a private matter became a security file

The facts so far are bad, and they are bad in an unusual way.

This week Huang admitted, in a letter to staff, to an extramarital relationship with Alice Huang (no relation), a former senior executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based AI infrastructure firm. U.S. intelligence reportedly believes she was working for Beijing. China denies it. Megaspeed reportedly bought $4.8 billion of Nvidia chips over two years. U.S. and Singaporean authorities have both investigated it over suspected diversion of chips to China, and the Justice Department has reportedly taken over the federal case.

Then on Wednesday the *New York Times*, citing five Nvidia employees, reported a second affair, with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's VP of healthcare and one of Huang's direct reports. Both are married. Sources say the two were filmed being affectionate at a San Jose hotel during GTC in March. According to the *Times*, Huang's security team "sequestered" the person who filmed it and offered them a nondisclosure agreement.

House Foreign Affairs Chair Brian Mast, China Committee Chair John Moolenaar and Intelligence Chair Rick Crawford have asked Huang to attend a closed-door briefing and say a subpoena is ready if he doesn't come. The board has hired Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz and former U.S. Attorney Joon H. Kim to investigate. The *Wall Street Journal* reports the board may act before that investigation is finished.

Each of these problems is serious on its own. Together they cover sex, national security and a possible cover-up, the three things a board can't simply wait out.

## The precedent is clear, and it points one way

Corporate America has a well-worn playbook here, and it is stricter than people remember.

- **Brian Krzanich, Intel, 2018:** resigned over a consensual relationship with an employee years earlier. No national-security angle and no cover-up allegation.
- **Steve Easterbrook, McDonald's, 2019:** fired over a consensual relationship with an employee. When the board later found he had lied about other relationships, it sued him, and he returned more than $105 million in 2021.
- **Mark Hurd, HP, 2010:** pushed out over a relationship with a contractor and related expense reports. HP's stock fell about 10% the next day, then recovered.

The relationship with Powell alone is a Krzanich-level violation. Nvidia's own code of conduct flags personal relationships as conflicts of interest. The hotel NDA is an Easterbrook-level problem, because it suggests the board was managed instead of informed. And none of those earlier CEOs was also explaining a relationship with a suspected foreign intelligence asset tied to a nine-figure-a-quarter customer.

The bull case for keeping Huang is that none of those men was Jensen Huang. That's true, and it doesn't help him. "He's irreplaceable" is exactly the argument that kept the board from planning for this moment.

## What nobody is talking about: the KYC problem

Most coverage treats the Alice Huang connection as a personal security risk: what did she learn, and what did she pass along? The bigger exposure is regulatory, and it is about how export controls actually work.

U.S. chip export rules depend heavily on the exporter's own diligence: know your customer, flag red-flag buyers, certify end users. For years, Nvidia's defense against smuggling allegations has been some version of "we follow the rules and can't police every downstream reseller." In fiscal 2025, Singapore accounted for roughly 18% of Nvidia's revenue by billing location, though Nvidia said actual shipments there were a small fraction of that. Singapore was already the most uncomfortable line in Nvidia's geographic disclosures before any of this.

Now consider how that defense sounds when the CEO had a personal relationship with a senior executive of a Singapore buyer under investigation for diversion. Every KYC judgment involving Megaspeed, and arguably every one involving Southeast Asian intermediaries, has a credibility problem. That doesn't mean anyone at Nvidia did anything wrong. It means Commerce and DOJ will no longer accept Nvidia's word without checking.

Here's the second-order effect. Expect tighter licensing in Southeast Asia, stricter end-use verification, and possibly third-party audits of Nvidia's distribution channels. Nvidia has already absorbed a $4.5 billion charge from the 2025 H20 restrictions and agreed to an unusual arrangement giving Washington a 15% cut of some China sales. Its political capital in Washington was already used up. This scandal draws down what little was left.

## The succession vacuum is the real governance failure

Nvidia has a deep bench of operators. CFO Colette Kress runs the financial story Wall Street trusts. Jay Puri runs global field operations. Debora Shoquist runs operations and supply chain. But "deep bench" isn't a succession plan. None of them has been publicly positioned as the next CEO, and none has been the public face of Nvidia's product vision, its CUDA platform strategy or its keynotes, where the company's market story is told.

That matters because Huang has never been just a manager. He decides product roadmaps, personally handles relationships with TSMC and the hyperscalers, and his yearly GTC and Computex keynotes are effectively Nvidia's investor relations. Compare Apple in 2011: Steve Jobs had been grooming Tim Cook in public for years, with Cook running the company during Jobs's medical leaves. When the handoff came, the market already knew who was next. Nvidia has no Tim Cook.

The Disney case shows the risk of improvising instead. Bob Chapek was named Bob Iger's successor in 2020, lasted under three years, and Iger came back. A rushed, scandal-driven pick at Nvidia could go the same way.

## The bear case for firing him

The case against ousting Huang deserves a fair hearing. The OpenAI board fired Sam Altman in November 2023 over governance concerns and reversed course within five days, after employees threatened to leave en masse and Microsoft offered to hire them. Nvidia's engineers are among the most heavily recruited people in tech, and many hold large amounts of stock. A founder pushed out by a board in a hurry could become a rallying point.

Huang also owns roughly 3.5% of the company, which makes him by far its largest individual shareholder. He can't block a board vote on his own, but he can make an ugly exit uglier.

Even so, the Altman comparison breaks down on one point. OpenAI's board never clearly explained why it fired Altman. Nvidia's board has a congressional subpoena threat, a DOJ investigation into a major customer, a code-of-conduct violation and an alleged witness NDA. Unlike OpenAI's board, it can explain itself in one paragraph.

## What this means for AI

For hyperscalers, a leadership change at Nvidia is short-term noise. Blackwell and Rubin roadmaps are already locked with TSMC, and nobody cancels GPU orders because of a scandal. Over the medium term, though, it strengthens every argument for custom silicon. Google's TPUs, Amazon's Trainium and Microsoft's Maia have always been pitched partly as insurance against dependence on one supplier. Now that insurance covers people as well as products.

AMD gets the cleanest opportunity. Lisa Su looks like a very boring CEO next to Huang right now, and in procurement meetings, boring sells.

## What to Watch

1. **The board acts before Wachtell finishes.** I expect an interim CEO announcement within two weeks. The most likely structure is Colette Kress as interim CEO, with Huang moved to a non-executive or "founder" role, the same face-saving formula boards use when they can't call a firing a firing.
2. **Huang testifies, in private.** He'll agree to the closed-door briefing before a subpoena is issued. Fighting Congress while the board is deciding his future would be unforced error number four.
3. **Commerce tightens rules on Southeast Asia within 90 days.** Expect new end-use verification requirements for AI chip exports to Singapore and Malaysia, with the Megaspeed case cited directly.
4. **Shareholder suits shift to Caremark claims.** The first lawsuits are about the stock drop. The more dangerous ones will ask what the board knew, especially since Wired reports Huang hired Heller, a crisis-communications firm known for sex scandals, last year. If directors knew, they're exposed.
5. **The NDA becomes its own story.** Whoever signed it and whoever approved it will be the next headline. If it was paid for with company resources, expect calls for clawbacks in the style of the McDonald's case against Easterbrook.

Nvidia will survive this. Its products are too far ahead and its customers too locked in. What won't survive is the idea that the world's most strategic AI company can be run on a founder's charisma without a backup. That idea was always shaky, and this week it collapsed.
