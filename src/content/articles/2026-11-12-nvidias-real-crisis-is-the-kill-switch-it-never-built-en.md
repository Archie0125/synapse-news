Jensen Huang’s resignation is the first responsible thing Nvidia’s board has done in this scandal—and it arrived years too late. The problem is not simply that Huang admitted to an affair with a woman US officials reportedly suspected of working for Chinese intelligence. It is that a company controlling the most valuable bottleneck in artificial intelligence apparently operated as if its founder were too important to constrain, replace, or seriously question.

That assumption was expensive. Nvidia lost more than $470 billion in market value over two days, according to [Engadget’s account of the shareholder lawsuit](https://www.engadget.com/2312947/nvidia-shareholders-are-suing-the-company-and-ceo-jensen-huang/). At an exchange rate of roughly NT$32 to the dollar, that is about NT$15 trillion erased—not because a GPU stopped working, but because investors suddenly discovered Nvidia’s governance architecture was weaker than its chips.

The numbers explain why this is more than Silicon Valley gossip. Nvidia generated $130.5 billion in fiscal 2025 revenue, up 114 percent in one year. Its data-center business alone produced $115.2 billion, or 88 percent of total revenue. Blackwell products brought in $11 billion during their first full quarter of sales. Nvidia is no longer just a semiconductor vendor; it is the toll booth through which Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, and dozens of sovereign AI projects must pass.

That makes the CEO a counterintelligence target by definition.

Huang did not need to hand over a Blackwell blueprint for a foreign intelligence operation to succeed. Information about shipment priorities, product delays, customer negotiations, export-control workarounds, and the timing of new China-specific products could be enormously valuable. Even a list of which cloud providers were receiving scarce systems first would help an adversary map America’s future compute capacity.

This is why the sex-scandal framing is so inadequate. The affair may be personal. The access was institutional.

## The founder problem finally met the national-security problem

Huang cofounded Nvidia in 1993 and remained its only CEO for more than 33 years. That continuity helped Nvidia survive failed products, pivot from gaming to accelerated computing, and build CUDA into a software moat competitors still struggle to cross. But the same mythology discouraged the board from constructing a visible succession plan.

Compare Nvidia with Apple. When Steve Jobs resigned on August 24, 2011, Tim Cook became permanent CEO that same day after years of running operations. Nvidia instead appointed CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO. Kress is a credible operator who joined in 2013, when Nvidia’s annual revenue was barely above $4 billion. She helped oversee its expansion into a company with more than 30 times that revenue and has spent years answering Wall Street’s hardest questions.

But knowing the financial model is not the same as owning the product road map. Nvidia now needs someone who can arbitrate between Blackwell production, Rubin development, CUDA, networking, export controls, hyperscaler allocations, and relationships with TSMC, SK Hynix, Micron, and server manufacturers. Kress may be able to do that. The board’s decision to label her “interim” tells customers and employees that it has not decided.

That ambiguity is dangerous because Nvidia’s products operate on a different clock from its scandal. GPU architectures are planned years ahead. CoWoS packaging capacity, high-bandwidth memory, power systems, and data-center construction are committed quarters before delivery. A leadership freeze today can become a supply distortion in 2027.

## Here’s what nobody is talking about: decision provenance

The key question is not only what Huang disclosed. It is whether Nvidia can reconstruct every business exception connected to Alice Huang, Megaspeed, and related intermediaries.

Megaspeed reportedly appeared through addresses leading to a Malaysian data center and shopping mall, a nearly empty Singapore office, and a deteriorating storefront near Kuala Lumpur. Those are not normal signals for the largest Southeast Asian buyer of the world’s most coveted AI accelerators. If Nvidia’s compliance systems flagged those inconsistencies, who overruled them? If they did not, why not? If executives approved unusual credit terms, allocations, or customer classifications, are those decisions documented?

This is a database problem disguised as a morality play.

A serious investigation should trace customer onboarding, beneficial ownership, shipping destinations, serial numbers, payment flows, internal messages, sales overrides, and executive contacts. If Huang’s relationship had no influence on Nvidia’s decisions, that record is his best defense. If the record is incomplete, the governance failure extends well beyond one executive.

The discrepancy around China makes this audit unavoidable. Huang said in May that Nvidia’s market share in China had fallen to zero because of US export policy. Short seller Culper Research, whose incentives should be treated skeptically, alleged that more than 20 percent of Nvidia’s fiscal 2026 compute revenue still came from China through diversion and Southeast Asian intermediaries. Zero percent and more than 20 percent are not competing interpretations of the same data. They describe two different companies.

Washington created this cat-and-mouse game. The October 2022 controls restricted Nvidia’s most capable chips; Nvidia responded with China-focused A800 and H800 models. The US tightened the rules in October 2023, and Nvidia later sold the lower-powered H20. Each round gave Nvidia billions of reasons to preserve Chinese demand while the Commerce Department tried to limit Chinese compute. A founder who publicly opposed export controls while privately maintaining questionable relationships was therefore not merely a reputational risk. He sat at the point where commercial incentives and national strategy collided.

## The lawsuit may hurt more than the affair

Shareholder Mark Kistenmacher’s proposed class action focuses on Nvidia’s statements during the period from October 9 to November 8. Nvidia initially characterized the Megaspeed connection as routine business contact. Huang later acknowledged the affair, and the company announced an investigation into whether he had been targeted by a foreign intelligence operation.

Securities law does not punish executives for marital infidelity. It punishes materially misleading disclosures. The billion-dollar question is when Nvidia’s board, lawyers, security team, and communications staff learned that the relationship was not routine—and why investors heard a different story.

The reported second affair involving subordinate Kimberly Powell may have given directors a clean corporate-conduct basis to force Huang out. But it looks more like the exit ramp than the underlying crash. Boards tolerate personal misconduct until it threatens control, disclosure, or the company’s license to operate. Nvidia’s directors acted when the national-security story made Huang a liability to Washington.

His planned testimony before Congress confirms that this has moved beyond corporate discipline. Huang agreed to appear by Thanksgiving after pressure from House Foreign Affairs Committee chair Brian Mast, China committee chair John Moolenaar, and Intelligence Committee chair Rick Crawford. The session will reportedly be closed, recorded, and transcribed. That may protect classified information, but secrecy will not restore public confidence unless Congress releases a meaningful account of what was learned.

## The counterargument: Nvidia is not one leather jacket

There is a strong case that the operational damage will be limited. Nvidia has thousands of engineers, a mature architecture process, long-term supply agreements, and customers that cannot replace CUDA overnight. TSMC will keep fabricating chips; SK Hynix and Micron will keep shipping memory; Foxconn, Quanta, and other manufacturers will keep assembling systems. Kress is not an outsider learning the business from a slide deck.

The market may also be overreacting to allegations whose full evidentiary basis has not been made public. Culper is a short seller. Intelligence suspicions are not criminal convictions. China-related revenue routed through Southeast Asia does not automatically prove sanctions evasion.

But that defense only reinforces the need for records. Nvidia cannot ask investors to trust a founder-centric culture after the founder became the disputed fact at the center of the investigation.

The second-order effect will be diversification. Google has TPUs, Amazon has Trainium, Microsoft has Maia, and Meta has MTIA. None currently matches Nvidia’s full combination of hardware, networking, and CUDA software. Yet every hyperscaler CFO now has a fresh argument for moving another five or ten percentage points of future AI infrastructure spending toward internal silicon. Nvidia will not lose its kingdom next quarter. It can lose pricing power one budget cycle at a time.

## What to Watch

- **A public investigation framework by December 2026.** Nvidia should name outside counsel, define the inquiry’s scope, and promise a nonclassified report. Silence beyond year-end will be interpreted as containment, not diligence.

- **A permanent leadership structure by the first quarter of 2027.** The most likely outcome is Kress as CEO supported by a technical president with explicit authority over architecture, CUDA, networking, and security. Hiring a celebrity outsider would waste six months Nvidia does not have.

- **Tighter customer verification in Southeast Asia by mid-2027.** Expect US rules targeting beneficial ownership, data-center locations, remote GPU access, and serial-number tracking in Singapore, Malaysia, and other transshipment hubs.

- **No immediate Blackwell supply collapse—but more double-ordering.** Large customers will reserve extra Nvidia capacity while accelerating TPU, Trainium, Maia, and MTIA programs. That will make demand look stronger in early 2027 and less dependable afterward.

- **Congress will force more disclosure than Nvidia wants.** If Huang’s closed-door testimony identifies compliance failures, lawmakers will demand either a transcript summary or new reporting obligations for advanced-chip vendors.

Nvidia spent three decades proving that accelerated computing could reorganize the technology industry. It now has to prove something less glamorous and more difficult: that the world’s most important AI infrastructure company can function without treating one man as its operating system.
