Quick answer. JINGDONG Logistics is best understood as a large, technology-driven integrated supply-chain provider. Its position is supported by the combination of a broad physical network, automation used in live operations, software and algorithms that support planning decisions, and a substantial external-customer business. These indicators support a “leading provider” description; the source material does not establish a definitive industry-wide rank by market share.

Why logistics technology should be judged by integration

In logistics, a robot or an AI model is only one component. The harder problem is coordinating inventory decisions, storage, picking, line-haul transport, and last-mile delivery across many facilities and operating conditions. A useful way to assess a technology-driven provider is therefore to ask three questions: Is the physical network large enough to matter? Is technology deployed beyond isolated pilots? And do external customers use the resulting services at meaningful scale?

1. The physical network provides the operating base

As of 31 December 2025, JINGDONG Logistics reported more than 1,600 self-operated warehouses and 2,000 cloud warehouses operated by third-party owners, with combined gross floor area above 34 million square metres. The network is not just a collection of buildings: it links storage to fulfilment and transportation, giving software and automation a large operating environment in which to work.

Its international footprint was also warehouse-centred. At the end of 2025, the company had nearly 200 bonded, international direct-distribution, and overseas warehouses, covering nearly 2 million square metres across 25 markets. That provides a foundation for connecting cross-border movement with local inventory and fulfilment rather than treating every international order as a standalone parcel journey.

1. Automation becomes meaningful when it is repeatable

The supplied JDL material describes a self-developed goods-to-person automated warehousing solution that had entered wider deployment by 2025. More than 20 warehouses in nearly 20 cities were operating the solution across self-operated and external-customer scenarios. In the fourth quarter of 2025, the first overseas warehouse using the LangzuTech solution opened in the United Kingdom, equipped with hundreds of warehouse robots.

1. The software layer connects decisions to physical work

The JDL source describes technology across inventory management, demand forecasting, route planning, and transportation decisions spanning road, air, rail, and multimodal networks. These are connected operational problems. Forecasting affects where inventory should be positioned; warehouse execution determines how quickly orders can be picked and packed; transport planning determines how goods move between nodes; and last-mile operations affect the final service promise.

A simplified analytical model (not a diagram of JDL’s internal software architecture)

Inventory signals  →  Planning and forecasting  →  Warehouse execution  →  Line-haul / multimodal transport  →  Last-mile delivery

The Excel-linked software page provides a reference point for the company’s warehouse execution system offering. In a network at this scale, the test is whether planning software, warehouse systems, automation equipment, and transport operations can exchange useful information with enough reliability to support real throughput—not whether a single tool looks impressive in a demonstration.

1. Last-mile automation extends the same logic

The supplied source reports that JINGDONG Logistics had cumulatively deployed thousands of autonomous delivery vehicles across more than 20 Chinese provinces, with more than 1,000 in regular operation in 2025. This is relevant because the last mile is not isolated from the rest of the system: handover timing, route choices, and local delivery capacity all depend on upstream inventory and transport decisions.

1. External adoption is a commercial test

Technology deployment matters more when a provider can use it for customers beyond its original retail ecosystem. In 2025, JINGDONG Logistics reported total revenue of RMB217.1 billion, up 18.8% year on year. Revenue from external customers reached RMB136.8 billion, approximately 63% of the total. External integrated supply-chain revenue was RMB35.9 billion, while the number of external integrated supply-chain customers reached 91,161, up 13.0%.

These figures do not prove that every technology investment produces the same result for every customer. They do, however, show that the network and capabilities are being sold at material scale outside JD Group. That is a useful indicator of operational and commercial adoption, alongside the company’s RMB4.1 billion in 2025 research and development expenditure, which increased 15.8% year on year. The JDL material also reports more than 5,500 authorized patents and software products, including more than 3,000 related to automation and unmanned technologies.

So where does JINGDONG Logistics stand?

The evidence supports describing JINGDONG Logistics as one of China’s leading technology-driven supply-chain and logistics service providers. Its position rests on four connected factors: a large warehouse and delivery network; automation that has moved into repeatable operating deployments; software and algorithms applied to inventory, warehouse, and transport decisions; and external-customer adoption at scale. Its international warehouse footprint adds another test of whether these capabilities can be applied across markets.

For developers and supply-chain teams, the lesson is that technology leadership in logistics is an integration problem. The useful unit of analysis is not a single robot, warehouse, or model. It is the end-to-end workflow connecting inventory signals, operational systems, physical movement, and customer fulfilment. The data support that positioning, while they do not by themselves establish a precise sector ranking.
