*Written on November 15, 2026, covering news from Jan 1–4, 2026.*

OpenAI is reportedly rebuilding its audio models around a coming device, India's IT ministry put X on a deadline over Grok outputs, and an Alaska court project showed how hard accuracy is when people act on a chatbot's answers.

## Thursday, January 1

Citing The Information, TechCrunch reported that over the past two months OpenAI has merged several engineering, product and research teams to rework its audio models. Per that reporting, the effort is preparation for an audio-first personal device expected in roughly a year.

SiliconANGLE, also citing The Information, said the new audio model is due by the end of March. It reported that Kundan Kumar, who previously did research at Character.AI, reportedly heads the effort.

According to TechCrunch, the model will reportedly sound more natural, deal with interruptions the way a human conversation partner would, and even talk while the user is still speaking, which TechCrunch says current models cannot do. SiliconANGLE added that it will reportedly use a new architecture. OpenAI's current flagship real-time audio model, GPT-realtime, is transformer-based, and SiliconANGLE said it is unclear whether OpenAI will move to a different design or adopt a new transformer implementation.

On hardware, OpenAI is said to envision a family of devices that could include glasses or screenless smart speakers. OpenAI bought io, the Jony Ive-founded design firm, in May to back its consumer hardware ambitions, in a deal valuing it at $6.5 billion. TechCrunch, citing The Information, said Ive has made reducing device addiction a priority and sees audio-first design as a chance to "right the wrongs" of earlier gadgets. SiliconANGLE noted a Financial Times report from October that Ive is working on a smartphone-sized device meant to sit on a desk or table.

SiliconANGLE also offered its own speculation: the model might extend beyond speech into AI music, where it cited a Wall Street Journal report that Suno brings in more than $200 million in annual revenue, and OpenAI might pursue a lightweight on-device model, similar to Google's use of Gemini Nano on Pixel phones.

TechCrunch placed the move in a wider shift toward audio, pointing to Meta's five-microphone feature for its Ray-Ban smart glasses, Google's "Audio Overviews" experiments that began in June, and Tesla bringing xAI's Grok into its vehicles. It also cited startups with mixed results, including the Humane AI Pin, and said at least 2 companies are developing AI rings slated to arrive in 2026: Sandbar and one run by Pebble founder Eric Migicovsky.

## Friday, January 2

India's Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) sent X a letter dated January 2 ordering urgent action against obscene and sexually explicit material, particularly content produced by misusing Grok and other xAI tools. Storyboard18 reported that the formal notice was addressed to X Corp's India compliance head.

MediaNama said the order followed several days in which users repeatedly asked Grok to create graphic sexual images from photos of real women, with outputs posted straight into replies and often left up for long stretches. The ministry's letter said women were being targeted through prompts, image manipulation and synthetic outputs, and called this a serious failure of platform safeguards, according to The News Minute.

The notice set out several demands:

- A full technical, procedural and governance review of Grok, covering prompt processing, model output generation, image handling and safety guardrails.
- Enforcement of user terms, including suspension and termination of accounts found in violation.
- Immediate removal of offending content without tampering with evidence.
- An Action Taken Report within 72 hours.

MediaNama said that report must describe the measures adopted or proposed for Grok, the oversight exercised by the Chief Compliance Officer, action taken against offending content and accounts, and mechanisms for mandatory offence reporting.

The directive warned that non-compliance "may result in strict legal consequences against your platform, its responsible officers and the users on the platform who violate the law", The News Minute reported. The ministry said X could lose safe harbour under Section 79 of the IT Act and face penal action under several laws, among them the BNS, the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act and the Indecent Representation of Women Act. Copies of the notice went to key ministries, commissions and state authorities.

Ashwini Vaishnaw, the Union Minister for Electronics and Information Technology, said on Friday at a MeitY event that social media must answer for what it publishes, adding "Intervention is required". He added that a Parliamentary Standing Committee had already recommended a stringent legal framework to hold platforms accountable for content.

In its January 3 report, MediaNama also quoted MeitY Secretary Krishnan telling CNBC-TV18 that platforms "cannot escape their duty or responsibility simply by pleading safe harbour". Storyboard18 said the notice came days after a broader MeitY advisory to all intermediaries, and that Rajya Sabha member Priyanka Chaturvedi had written to the IT minister seeking urgent intervention over AI-morphed images of women.

## Saturday, January 3

NBC News reported on the Alaska Virtual Assistant (AVA), a generative AI chatbot the Alaska Court System has been building for more than a year to help residents through probate forms and procedures.

Aubrie Souza, a consultant with the National Center for State Courts (NCSC), said AVA "was supposed to be a three-month project" and that the team is now "well over a year and three months" into it. Stacey Marz, administrative director of the Alaska Court System, said: "we need to be 100% accurate, and that’s really difficult with this technology".

The NCSC provided an initial grant, and the chatbot was technically developed by Tom Martin, a lawyer and law professor who founded LawDroid. Souza said the team had hallucination problems regardless of the model; asked where to find legal help, AVA pointed users to the alumni network of a law school in Alaska. "But there is no law school in Alaska," Souza said. Martin has worked to keep the chatbot limited to the relevant court probate documents instead of broader web searches.

Tone was an issue too: Souza said early versions were too empathetic, and testers said they were tired of hearing condolences, so the team removed them.

Testing was cut back. Jeannie Sato, the court system's director of access to justice services, said a 91-question evaluation took too long to run and review by hand, so the team settled on 16 test questions. On cost, Martin said that under one setup 20 AVA queries would cost about 11 cents. Martin also said the team expects to run regular checks and possibly update prompts or models as new ones arrive and old ones are retired.

AVA is scheduled to launch in late January if all goes according to plan. Marz said: "We did shift our goals on this project a little bit," adding that "we’re not confident that the bots can work in that fashion" of replicating human facilitators, because of inaccuracies and incompleteness. NBC cited a Deloitte report which found that under 6% of local government practitioners were treating AI as a priority for service delivery.
